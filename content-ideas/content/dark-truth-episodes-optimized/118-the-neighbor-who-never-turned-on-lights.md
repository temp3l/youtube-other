# Narration Script

My neighbor lived in complete darkness, but every night I saw him standing at the window.

Maya Rill was a translator working from home, the sort of practical person who checked locks, logs, and timestamps before trusting rumors. At first, an apartment courtyard with one always-dark window felt like a maintenance problem, not a haunting. Old structures complained. Bad wiring made patterns. Witnesses turned ordinary fear into warnings. Then Maya heard the rule that made every later detail harder to dismiss: Do not wave to someone standing in a dark window.

The first sign was a thin white curtain. It was not hidden. It sat in the open, clean and deliberate, with no broken lock, no muddy scramble around it, and no person on the nearest camera. Under the rain, dust, or silence of the place, Maya kept noticing the same three sounds: curtain rings, distant apartment pipes, and fingernails on glass. At first they belonged to the building. Later, they sounded like instructions.

Maya handled it like an incident report. Photos were taken from fixed angles. Times were written down. Doors, windows, meters, locks, and storage spaces were checked twice. Each note had a practical label: draft, old hardware, bad sensor, tired eyes. The first ordinary explanation lasted only until the next check revealed building records showing the opposite apartment had been empty for months. That was the first moment the fear became specific. It was not a scream in the dark. It was a documented contradiction, and every documented contradiction was harder to forget.

After that, the dark-window neighbor stopped behaving like a random disturbance. Each time Maya tested one detail, another detail answered. A lock returned closed after being photographed open. A reflection showed movement before the room moved. A sound that should have stayed behind Maya came from ahead. The object from the first sign never reappeared in the same position twice, yet every photo insisted it had always been there. The rule no longer sounded like superstition. It sounded like the only surviving instruction from someone who had already failed.

Leaving would have been the sensible choice: lock up, send the notes, call for help, and wait for daylight. Maya almost did. But the contradiction — building records showing the opposite apartment had been empty for months — meant the danger was not just atmospheric. It selected people, repeated steps, and used proof as bait. If Maya walked away with only half the pattern understood, the next practical person would enter the same scene with less warning.

The oldest record made it worse: Tenants who waved all reported seeing the figure on lower floors afterward. That detail did not explain everything, but it connected too many points to ignore. Earlier witnesses had measured, argued, documented, and resisted. Their mistake was not stupidity. Their mistake was believing that once the pattern had been named, it could be controlled.

Then the danger became personal. The neighbor copied Maya's wave, then her posture, then her exact clothing. The terror worked because it was exact. It knew which sound could freeze a hand above a switch, which memory could make caution feel cruel, and which urgent message could make obedience seem cowardly. Maya stopped trying to win. The goal became smaller: survive the next minute without giving the place a cleaner invitation.

The second confirmation came in pieces, which made it worse. One timestamp matched. One direction matched. One small sensory detail matched the warning exactly. Described out loud, it sounded theatrical. Seen in order, it was painfully physical: first a thin white curtain; then building records showing the opposite apartment had been empty for months; then the neighbor copied Maya's wave, then her posture, then her exact clothing. The impossible did not arrive as one grand reveal. It built itself from ordinary evidence until ordinary evidence could no longer hold it.

The first real plan failed because it was reasonable. She taped the curtains shut, avoided the courtyard, and asked management to check the unit. At first, the setup held. The checks were clean. The boundaries looked normal enough to trust. Then the breach appeared from the wrong side of the precaution, without disturbing the thing the precaution was meant to protect. That was when Maya understood the trap: the danger did not punish recklessness. It punished confidence.

The opposite window stayed blank and no silhouette returned. The silence afterward was almost convincing. It gave Maya enough room to feel foolish and to imagine a report made of plain words: stress, weather, fatigue, faulty equipment. Even the shadows settled into normal shapes for a while. Then the motif returned — curtain rings, distant apartment pipes, and fingernails on glass — not louder, just closer. No one else would have called it a scream. By then, Maya understood that subtle sounds were how the place announced ownership.

The final attempt depended on obeying the rule exactly, not heroically. No bargaining. No challenge. No last look for better proof. Every movement had to stay small, boring, and disciplined. That restraint almost worked. Then the thing offered the one reason most likely to break it: a familiar voice, a visible rescue, a message urgent enough to override memory. Maya waited until the impulse passed. In that pause, the truth became visible.

The final verification removed the last ordinary explanation. Management sent a photo from the empty unit showing Maya standing at her own window. A single camera could still be doubted. A timestamp could be blamed on hardware. A witness could be dismissed as tired. Together, the pieces formed proof, but not comfort. They agreed on the one fact Maya most wanted to reject: the rule had never been meant to save everyone. It had only been meant to decide who would be noticed last.

By morning, the official version was smaller than the truth. It mentioned weather, stress, equipment failure, and a misread warning. It left out the moment Maya understood that survival had not ended the pattern. It had made Maya part of the pattern. The place, the object, the sound, and the rule remained connected, waiting for the next careful person to test them.

In the morning, a handprint appeared on the inside of her curtain.
