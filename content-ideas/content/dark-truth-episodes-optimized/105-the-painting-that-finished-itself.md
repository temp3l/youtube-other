# Narration Script

Every morning, the unfinished painting had one more detail—and the details were from inside my apartment.

Elian Crow was an art restorer, the sort of practical person who checked locks, logs, and timestamps before trusting rumors. At first, a restoration studio above a closed gallery felt like a maintenance problem, not a haunting. Old structures complained. Bad wiring made patterns. Witnesses turned ordinary fear into warnings. Then Elian heard the rule that made every later detail harder to dismiss: Never let the painting include your face.

The first sign was an unfinished oil portrait. It was not hidden. It sat in the open, clean and deliberate, with no broken lock, no muddy scramble around it, and no person on the nearest camera. Under the rain, dust, or silence of the place, Elian kept noticing the same three sounds: wet brush strokes, canvas tension, and rain against skylights. At first they belonged to the building. Later, they sounded like instructions.

Elian handled it like an incident report. Photos were taken from fixed angles. Times were written down. Doors, windows, meters, locks, and storage spaces were checked twice. Each note had a practical label: draft, old hardware, bad sensor, tired eyes. The first ordinary explanation lasted only until the next check revealed fresh paint showing Elian asleep beside a lamp he had bought the same day. That was the first moment the fear became specific. It was not a scream in the dark. It was a documented contradiction, and every documented contradiction was harder to forget.

After that, the woman in the black garden stopped behaving like a random disturbance. Each time Elian tested one detail, another detail answered. A lock returned closed after being photographed open. A reflection showed movement before the room moved. A sound that should have stayed behind Elian came from ahead. The object from the first sign never reappeared in the same position twice, yet every photo insisted it had always been there. The rule no longer sounded like superstition. It sounded like the only surviving instruction from someone who had already failed.

Leaving would have been the sensible choice: lock up, send the notes, call for help, and wait for daylight. Elian almost did. But the contradiction — fresh paint showing Elian asleep beside a lamp he had bought the same day — meant the danger was not just atmospheric. It selected people, repeated steps, and used proof as bait. If Elian walked away with only half the pattern understood, the next practical person would enter the same scene with less warning.

The oldest record made it worse: The portrait's previous restorers all disappeared after finishing the background figure. That detail did not explain everything, but it connected too many points to ignore. Earlier witnesses had measured, argued, documented, and resisted. Their mistake was not stupidity. Their mistake was believing that once the pattern had been named, it could be controlled.

Then the danger became personal. The painted woman slowly turned her head toward Elian's side of the room. The terror worked because it was exact. It knew which sound could freeze a hand above a switch, which memory could make caution feel cruel, and which urgent message could make obedience seem cowardly. Elian stopped trying to win. The goal became smaller: survive the next minute without giving the place a cleaner invitation.

The second confirmation came in pieces, which made it worse. One timestamp matched. One direction matched. One small sensory detail matched the warning exactly. Described out loud, it sounded theatrical. Seen in order, it was painfully physical: first an unfinished oil portrait; then fresh paint showing Elian asleep beside a lamp he had bought the same day; then the painted woman slowly turned her head toward Elian's side of the room. The impossible did not arrive as one grand reveal. It built itself from ordinary evidence until ordinary evidence could no longer hold it.

The first real plan failed because it was reasonable. He locked the canvas in a crate, aimed cameras at it, and refused to sleep in the studio. At first, the setup held. The checks were clean. The boundaries looked normal enough to trust. Then the breach appeared from the wrong side of the precaution, without disturbing the thing the precaution was meant to protect. That was when Elian understood the trap: the danger did not punish recklessness. It punished confidence.

The crate remained sealed and the cameras showed no movement. The silence afterward was almost convincing. It gave Elian enough room to feel foolish and to imagine a report made of plain words: stress, weather, fatigue, faulty equipment. Even the shadows settled into normal shapes for a while. Then the motif returned — wet brush strokes, canvas tension, and rain against skylights — not louder, just closer. No one else would have called it a scream. By then, Elian understood that subtle sounds were how the place announced ownership.

The final attempt depended on obeying the rule exactly, not heroically. No bargaining. No challenge. No last look for better proof. Every movement had to stay small, boring, and disciplined. That restraint almost worked. Then the thing offered the one reason most likely to break it: a familiar voice, a visible rescue, a message urgent enough to override memory. Elian waited until the impulse passed. In that pause, the truth became visible.

The final verification removed the last ordinary explanation. In the morning, every blank wall in his apartment carried one new painted branch. A single camera could still be doubted. A timestamp could be blamed on hardware. A witness could be dismissed as tired. Together, the pieces formed proof, but not comfort. They agreed on the one fact Elian most wanted to reject: the rule had never been meant to save everyone. It had only been meant to decide who would be noticed last.

By morning, the official version was smaller than the truth. It mentioned weather, stress, equipment failure, and a misread warning. It left out the moment Elian understood that survival had not ended the pattern. It had made Elian part of the pattern. The place, the object, the sound, and the rule remained connected, waiting for the next careful person to test them.

The portrait completed itself with Elian in the background, screaming without sound.
