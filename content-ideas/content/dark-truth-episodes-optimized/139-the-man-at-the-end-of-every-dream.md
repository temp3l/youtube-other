# Narration Script

I started seeing the same man at the end of every dream, always a little closer to waking up with me.

Dr. Elise Ward was a sleep clinic technician, the sort of practical person who checked locks, logs, and timestamps before trusting rumors. At first, a sleep laboratory and a recurring hallway inside dreams felt like a maintenance problem, not a haunting. Old structures complained. Bad wiring made patterns. Witnesses turned ordinary fear into warnings. Then Elise heard the rule that made every later detail harder to dismiss: Wake up before he reaches the door.

The first sign was a polysomnography monitor. It was not hidden. It sat in the open, clean and deliberate, with no broken lock, no muddy scramble around it, and no person on the nearest camera. Under the rain, dust, or silence of the place, Elise kept noticing the same three sounds: sleep monitor beeps, distant footsteps, and a bedroom door latch. At first they belonged to the building. Later, they sounded like instructions.

Elise handled it like an incident report. Photos were taken from fixed angles. Times were written down. Doors, windows, meters, locks, and storage spaces were checked twice. Each note had a practical label: draft, old hardware, bad sensor, tired eyes. The first ordinary explanation lasted only until the next check revealed sleep footage showing the dream figure standing beside patients' beds. That was the first moment the fear became specific. It was not a scream in the dark. It was a documented contradiction, and every documented contradiction was harder to forget.

After that, the man at the end stopped behaving like a random disturbance. Each time Elise tested one detail, another detail answered. A lock returned closed after being photographed open. A reflection showed movement before the room moved. A sound that should have stayed behind Elise came from ahead. The object from the first sign never reappeared in the same position twice, yet every photo insisted it had always been there. The rule no longer sounded like superstition. It sounded like the only surviving instruction from someone who had already failed.

Leaving would have been the sensible choice: lock up, send the notes, call for help, and wait for daylight. Elise almost did. But the contradiction — sleep footage showing the dream figure standing beside patients' beds — meant the danger was not just atmospheric. It selected people, repeated steps, and used proof as bait. If Elise walked away with only half the pattern understood, the next practical person would enter the same scene with less warning.

The oldest record made it worse: The study was based on an abandoned protocol designed to map the last dream before death. That detail did not explain everything, but it connected too many points to ignore. Earlier witnesses had measured, argued, documented, and resisted. Their mistake was not stupidity. Their mistake was believing that once the pattern had been named, it could be controlled.

Then the danger became personal. Elise dreamed of the hallway after hearing patients describe it word for word. The terror worked because it was exact. It knew which sound could freeze a hand above a switch, which memory could make caution feel cruel, and which urgent message could make obedience seem cowardly. Elise stopped trying to win. The goal became smaller: survive the next minute without giving the place a cleaner invitation.

The second confirmation came in pieces, which made it worse. One timestamp matched. One direction matched. One small sensory detail matched the warning exactly. Described out loud, it sounded theatrical. Seen in order, it was painfully physical: first a polysomnography monitor; then sleep footage showing the dream figure standing beside patients' beds; then elise dreamed of the hallway after hearing patients describe it word for word. The impossible did not arrive as one grand reveal. It built itself from ordinary evidence until ordinary evidence could no longer hold it.

The first real plan failed because it was reasonable. She ended the study, sedated no one, and kept every patient awake under bright lights. At first, the setup held. The checks were clean. The boundaries looked normal enough to trust. Then the breach appeared from the wrong side of the precaution, without disturbing the thing the precaution was meant to protect. That was when Elise understood the trap: the danger did not punish recklessness. It punished confidence.

The monitors showed stable waking brain activity. The silence afterward was almost convincing. It gave Elise enough room to feel foolish and to imagine a report made of plain words: stress, weather, fatigue, faulty equipment. Even the shadows settled into normal shapes for a while. Then the motif returned — sleep monitor beeps, distant footsteps, and a bedroom door latch — not louder, just closer. No one else would have called it a scream. By then, Elise understood that subtle sounds were how the place announced ownership.

The final attempt depended on obeying the rule exactly, not heroically. No bargaining. No challenge. No last look for better proof. Every movement had to stay small, boring, and disciplined. That restraint almost worked. Then the thing offered the one reason most likely to break it: a familiar voice, a visible rescue, a message urgent enough to override memory. Elise waited until the impulse passed. In that pause, the truth became visible.

The final verification removed the last ordinary explanation. One patient whispered from full wakefulness that the hallway was now on Elise's side. A single camera could still be doubted. A timestamp could be blamed on hardware. A witness could be dismissed as tired. Together, the pieces formed proof, but not comfort. They agreed on the one fact Elise most wanted to reject: the rule had never been meant to save everyone. It had only been meant to decide who would be noticed last.

By morning, the official version was smaller than the truth. It mentioned weather, stress, equipment failure, and a misread warning. It left out the moment Elise understood that survival had not ended the pattern. It had made Elise part of the pattern. The place, the object, the sound, and the rule remained connected, waiting for the next careful person to test them.

The man opened the dream door, and Elise woke to find her bedroom door already open.
