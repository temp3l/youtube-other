# Narration Script

Every coin Ellis dropped into the well came back covered in someone else's blood.

Ellis Grant was an overnight cashier working in a failing gas station on an old bypass road. The night should have been simple: verify cash-drawer footage, check the pump island, and leave one clean record for morning. At 11:34 p.m., Ellis noticed the old wishing well was wrong in a way that did not need belief. It did not begin with a scream. It began with a wrong timestamp, a shadow where no shadow belonged, and heat pressing on the room.

Ellis did what a careful person does first. A photo was taken. The time was written down. The nearest exit was kept open. Then Ellis tried to force the ordinary explanation to fit. Bad wiring, old pipes, a delayed feed, a prank, a corrupt file—each theory lasted only until the old wishing well changed again. The sound under it was unmistakable: neon hum, coins striking stone, and water echoing from far too deep underground. The sound did not grow louder. It grew more accurate.

Tara, the only other person reachable at that hour, told Ellis to stop testing it. That warning landed harder than panic, because Tara sounded tired, like someone repeating a rule learned too late. “Listen to me,” Tara said. “Never drop money into the well unless you know exactly what you are paying for.” Ellis laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear sometimes needs a smaller shape before the mind will hold it.

The second verification removed any comfort left in doubt. Cash-drawer footage showed the event starting before Ellis arrived. One image showed the pump island empty. The next showed the old wishing well waiting in the exact spot Ellis would later need to cross. The detail felt staged, but not for an audience. It felt staged for one decision. Whatever was present did not need to overpower anyone. It only needed a reasonable person to choose wrong.

Tara found the first matching report in a scanned folder labeled maintenance, although the handwriting on the last page looked fresh. That made the night feel less like an accident and more like a procedure. The event had a boundary. It had a timing. It reacted when named, measured, opened, watched, bought, counted, answered, or followed. The rule was not folklore. It was the surviving edge of somebody else's failure.

At 3:02 a.m., the situation became personal. Ellis's phone lit without ringing. The preview contained a sentence Ellis had said earlier in the night, but the sender was listed as Ellis. When the message opened, there was no text, only a short audio file. In it, Ellis's own voice whispered, “I already did what you are thinking about doing.” Then came neon hum, coins striking stone, and water echoing from far too deep underground, so faint it could have been inside the speaker.

The next call came from the property manager. The voice used correct names, correct addresses, and correct procedure. It said the danger was over, help had arrived, and the safest thing now was to interact with the old wishing well exactly once. That was the clever part. The instruction sounded like rescue, but it required the one action the rule had forbidden. Ellis asked the caller to repeat the case number. The line went quiet long enough for the breathing on the other end to stop pretending.

Ellis made a plan that would look sensible in a police report. Stop interacting with the old wishing well. Preserve the evidence. Move toward a public exit. Do not improvise. Do not answer. Do not touch the thing that wanted to be confirmed. For almost three minutes, it worked. The route out became ordinary again. The silence was so complete that it felt like permission to breathe.

Then the threat used the one voice that made caution feel cruel. Someone called from the wrong side of the pump island, using Tara's voice and saying exactly what a real person would say: that they were hurt, that the door was stuck, that Ellis was leaving them there. Ellis kept one hand against the wall and repeated the rule under their breath. That was when the old wishing well moved close enough to cast a shadow where no light source existed.

The escape depended on a small human detail the thing had not learned. Ellis left a false instruction where the pattern would find it, then moved without looking back to see whether it obeyed. For the first time, the event hesitated. Normal sound returned in pieces: a motor settling, air in a vent, rain or insects beyond glass, one distant vehicle moving through the real world. Ellis reached the exit with the evidence still recording.

The recording is the reason the incident was not dismissed completely. In the final minute, Ellis can be heard breathing hard and refusing to answer three separate times. After the third refusal, the microphone captures neon hum, coins striking stone, and water echoing from far too deep underground, followed by a second voice saying Ellis's name with the careful pronunciation of someone reading it from a file. Then the camera tilts down, though nobody admits touching it, and shows a thin line of wet dust leading away.

Morning did not make the case easier to explain. The report contained photographs, timestamps, and one damaged file, but no version of events that satisfied everyone. Officials preferred trespassers, stress, weather, faulty equipment, or sleep deprivation. The people who reviewed the evidence were less confident. In the final usable frame, Ellis was visible leaving the pump island, while another version of Ellis remained beside the old wishing well, perfectly still, as if waiting to be noticed again.

That is why the warning survived in practical language instead of superstition. No one close to the incident tells people to believe in ghosts. They tell them to respect the first impossible detail. They tell them not to test the rule for proof. And whenever neon hum, coins striking stone, and water echoing from far too deep underground returns in a place it should not be, they stop speaking until it passes.

Behind the gas station, the well was still counting change.
