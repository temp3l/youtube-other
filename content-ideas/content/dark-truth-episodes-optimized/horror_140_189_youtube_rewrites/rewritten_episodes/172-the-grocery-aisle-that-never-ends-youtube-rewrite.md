# Narration Script

Aisle thirteen did not exist until Paula walked past aisle twelve for the second time.

Paula Dean was an overnight grocery stocker working in a supermarket during inventory night. The night should have been simple: verify inventory scanner, check the repeating shelves, and leave one clean record for morning. At 11:04 p.m., Paula noticed aisle thirteen was wrong in a way that did not need belief. It did not begin with a scream. It began with a wrong timestamp, a shadow where no shadow belonged, and heat pressing on the room.

Paula did what a careful person does first. A photo was taken. The time was written down. The nearest exit was kept open. Then Paula tried to force the ordinary explanation to fit. Bad wiring, old pipes, a delayed feed, a prank, a corrupt file—each theory lasted only until aisle thirteen changed again. The sound under it was unmistakable: freezer hum, shopping cart wheels squeaking, and cans falling one by one in the distance. The sound did not grow louder. It grew more accurate.

Rowan, the only other person reachable at that hour, told Paula to stop testing it. That warning landed harder than panic, because Rowan sounded tired, like someone repeating a rule learned too late. “Listen to me,” Rowan said. “If an aisle has no end cap, turn around before the shelves repeat.” Paula laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear sometimes needs a smaller shape before the mind will hold it.

The second verification removed any comfort left in doubt. Inventory scanner showed the event starting before Paula arrived. One image showed the repeating shelves empty. The next showed aisle thirteen waiting in the exact spot Paula would later need to cross. The detail felt staged, but not for an audience. It felt staged for one decision. Whatever was present did not need to overpower anyone. It only needed a reasonable person to choose wrong.

In the archive, Paula found a complaint from 1989 with the same wording crossed out in blue pencil. The complainant had not asked for money. He had asked for his name to be removed from every copy. That made the night feel less like an accident and more like a procedure. The event had a boundary. It had a timing. It reacted when named, measured, opened, watched, bought, counted, answered, or followed. The rule was not folklore. It was the surviving edge of somebody else's failure.

At 1:32 a.m., the situation became personal. Paula's phone lit without ringing. The preview contained a sentence Paula had said earlier in the night, but the sender was listed as Paula. When the message opened, there was no text, only a short audio file. In it, Paula's own voice whispered, “I already did what you are thinking about doing.” Then came freezer hum, shopping cart wheels squeaking, and cans falling one by one in the distance, so faint it could have been inside the speaker.

The next call came from the property manager. The voice used correct names, correct addresses, and correct procedure. It said the danger was over, help had arrived, and the safest thing now was to interact with aisle thirteen exactly once. That was the clever part. The instruction sounded like rescue, but it required the one action the rule had forbidden. Paula asked the caller to repeat the case number. The line went quiet long enough for the breathing on the other end to stop pretending.

Paula made a plan that would look sensible in a police report. Stop interacting with aisle thirteen. Preserve the evidence. Move toward a public exit. Do not improvise. Do not answer. Do not touch the thing that wanted to be confirmed. For almost three minutes, it worked. The route out became ordinary again. The silence was so complete that it felt like permission to breathe.

Then the threat used the one voice that made caution feel cruel. Someone called from the wrong side of the repeating shelves, using Rowan's voice and saying exactly what a real person would say: that they were hurt, that the door was stuck, that Paula was leaving them there. Paula kept one hand against the wall and repeated the rule under their breath. That was when aisle thirteen moved close enough to cast a shadow where no light source existed.

The escape depended on a small human detail the thing had not learned. Paula left a false instruction where the pattern would find it, then moved without looking back to see whether it obeyed. For the first time, the event hesitated. Normal sound returned in pieces: a motor settling, air in a vent, rain or insects beyond glass, one distant vehicle moving through the real world. Paula reached the exit with the evidence still recording.

The recording is the reason the incident was not dismissed completely. In the final minute, Paula can be heard breathing hard and refusing to answer three separate times. After the third refusal, the microphone captures freezer hum, shopping cart wheels squeaking, and cans falling one by one in the distance, followed by a second voice saying Paula's name with the careful pronunciation of someone reading it from a file. Then the camera tilts down, though nobody admits touching it, and shows a thin line of wet dust leading away.

Morning did not make the case easier to explain. The report contained photographs, timestamps, and one damaged file, but no version of events that satisfied everyone. Officials preferred trespassers, stress, weather, faulty equipment, or sleep deprivation. The people who reviewed the evidence were less confident. In the final usable frame, Paula was visible leaving the repeating shelves, while another version of Paula remained beside aisle thirteen, perfectly still, as if waiting to be noticed again.

That is why the warning survived in practical language instead of superstition. No one close to the incident tells people to believe in ghosts. They tell them to respect the first impossible detail. They tell them not to test the rule for proof. And whenever freezer hum, shopping cart wheels squeaking, and cans falling one by one in the distance returns in a place it should not be, they stop speaking until it passes.

The store had not closed around her. It had stocked her.
