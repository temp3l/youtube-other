# Narration Script

The flooded church was full of dolls sitting in the pews where people should have been.

Marcus Vale was a disaster recovery photographer working in a drowned river town after levee failure. The night should have been simple: verify flood photos, check the front pew, and leave one clean record for morning. At 12:43 a.m., Marcus noticed the dolls was wrong in a way that did not need belief. It did not begin with a scream. It began with a wrong timestamp, a shadow where no shadow belonged, and distant traffic thinning into silence.

Marcus did what a careful person does first. A photo was taken. The time was written down. The nearest exit was kept open. Then Marcus tried to force the ordinary explanation to fit. Bad wiring, old pipes, a delayed feed, a prank, a corrupt file—each theory lasted only until the dolls changed again. The sound under it was unmistakable: water sloshing through pews, porcelain knocking together, and distant hymns under floodwater. The sound did not grow louder. It grew more accurate.

Graham, the only other person reachable at that hour, told Marcus to stop testing it. That warning landed harder than panic, because Graham sounded tired, like someone repeating a rule learned too late. “Listen to me,” Graham said. “If the dolls face the altar, leave before they turn around.” Marcus laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear sometimes needs a smaller shape before the mind will hold it.

The second verification removed any comfort left in doubt. Flood photos showed the event starting before Marcus arrived. One image showed the front pew empty. The next showed the dolls waiting in the exact spot Marcus would later need to cross. The detail felt staged, but not for an audience. It felt staged for one decision. Whatever was present did not need to overpower anyone. It only needed a reasonable person to choose wrong.

The oldest matching record was not filed as a haunting. It was filed as damaged equipment, missing time, and one witness who refused to give a second statement. That made the night feel less like an accident and more like a procedure. The event had a boundary. It had a timing. It reacted when named, measured, opened, watched, bought, counted, answered, or followed. The rule was not folklore. It was the surviving edge of somebody else's failure.

At 2:59 a.m., the situation became personal. Marcus's phone lit without ringing. The preview contained a sentence Marcus had said earlier in the night, but the sender was listed as Marcus. When the message opened, there was no text, only a short audio file. In it, Marcus's own voice whispered, “I already did what you are thinking about doing.” Then came water sloshing through pews, porcelain knocking together, and distant hymns under floodwater, so faint it could have been inside the speaker.

The next call came from the old emergency contact. The voice used correct names, correct addresses, and correct procedure. It said the danger was over, help had arrived, and the safest thing now was to interact with the dolls exactly once. That was the clever part. The instruction sounded like rescue, but it required the one action the rule had forbidden. Marcus asked the caller to repeat the case number. The line went quiet long enough for the breathing on the other end to stop pretending.

Marcus made a plan that would look sensible in a police report. Stop interacting with the dolls. Preserve the evidence. Move toward a public exit. Do not improvise. Do not answer. Do not touch the thing that wanted to be confirmed. For almost three minutes, it worked. The route out became ordinary again. The silence was so complete that it felt like permission to breathe.

Then the threat used the one voice that made caution feel cruel. Someone called from the wrong side of the front pew, using Graham's voice and saying exactly what a real person would say: that they were hurt, that the door was stuck, that Marcus was leaving them there. Marcus kept one hand against the wall and repeated the rule under their breath. That was when the dolls moved close enough to cast a shadow where no light source existed.

The escape depended on a small human detail the thing had not learned. Marcus left a false instruction where the pattern would find it, then moved without looking back to see whether it obeyed. For the first time, the event hesitated. Normal sound returned in pieces: a motor settling, air in a vent, rain or insects beyond glass, one distant vehicle moving through the real world. Marcus reached the exit with the evidence still recording.

The recording is the reason the incident was not dismissed completely. In the final minute, Marcus can be heard breathing hard and refusing to answer three separate times. After the third refusal, the microphone captures water sloshing through pews, porcelain knocking together, and distant hymns under floodwater, followed by a second voice saying Marcus's name with the careful pronunciation of someone reading it from a file. Then the camera tilts down, though nobody admits touching it, and shows a thin line of wet dust leading away.

Morning did not make the case easier to explain. The report contained photographs, timestamps, and one damaged file, but no version of events that satisfied everyone. Officials preferred trespassers, stress, weather, faulty equipment, or sleep deprivation. The people who reviewed the evidence were less confident. In the final usable frame, Marcus was visible leaving the front pew, while another version of Marcus remained beside the dolls, perfectly still, as if waiting to be noticed again.

That is why the warning survived in practical language instead of superstition. No one close to the incident tells people to believe in ghosts. They tell them to respect the first impossible detail. They tell them not to test the rule for proof. And whenever water sloshing through pews, porcelain knocking together, and distant hymns under floodwater returns in a place it should not be, they stop speaking until it passes.

In the final image, Marcus was already in the front pew.
