# Narration Script

Iris recorded one line of narration and heard another woman finish the sentence from inside the booth.

Iris Shaw was an audiobook narrator working in a rented basement recording studio below an old radio station. The night should have been simple: verify raw audio files, check the red recording light, and leave one clean record for morning. At 11:26 p.m., Iris noticed the recording booth was wrong in a way that did not need belief. It did not begin with a scream. It began with a wrong timestamp, a shadow where no shadow belonged, and cold air moving at ankle height.

Iris did what a careful person does first. A photo was taken. The time was written down. The nearest exit was kept open. Then Iris tried to force the ordinary explanation to fit. Bad wiring, old pipes, a delayed feed, a prank, a corrupt file—each theory lasted only until the recording booth changed again. The sound under it was unmistakable: headphone hiss, booth glass tapping, and a second voice under the waveform. The sound did not grow louder. It grew more accurate.

Eve, the only other person reachable at that hour, told Iris to stop testing it. That warning landed harder than panic, because Eve sounded tired, like someone repeating a rule learned too late. “Listen to me,” Eve said. “If the playback contains a sentence you did not read, do not record over it.” Iris laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear sometimes needs a smaller shape before the mind will hold it.

The second verification removed any comfort left in doubt. Raw audio files showed the event starting before Iris arrived. One image showed the red recording light empty. The next showed the recording booth waiting in the exact spot Iris would later need to cross. The detail felt staged, but not for an audience. It felt staged for one decision. Whatever was present did not need to overpower anyone. It only needed a reasonable person to choose wrong.

Eve found the first matching report in a scanned folder labeled maintenance, although the handwriting on the last page looked fresh. That made the night feel less like an accident and more like a procedure. The event had a boundary. It had a timing. It reacted when named, measured, opened, watched, bought, counted, answered, or followed. The rule was not folklore. It was the surviving edge of somebody else's failure.

At 3:58 a.m., the situation became personal. Iris's phone lit without ringing. The preview contained a sentence Iris had said earlier in the night, but the sender was listed as Iris. When the message opened, there was no text, only a short audio file. In it, Iris's own voice whispered, “I already did what you are thinking about doing.” Then came headphone hiss, booth glass tapping, and a second voice under the waveform, so faint it could have been inside the speaker.

The next call came from the automated service line. The voice used correct names, correct addresses, and correct procedure. It said the danger was over, help had arrived, and the safest thing now was to interact with the recording booth exactly once. That was the clever part. The instruction sounded like rescue, but it required the one action the rule had forbidden. Iris asked the caller to repeat the case number. The line went quiet long enough for the breathing on the other end to stop pretending.

Iris made a plan that would look sensible in a police report. Stop interacting with the recording booth. Preserve the evidence. Move toward a public exit. Do not improvise. Do not answer. Do not touch the thing that wanted to be confirmed. For almost three minutes, it worked. The route out became ordinary again. The silence was so complete that it felt like permission to breathe.

Then the threat used the one voice that made caution feel cruel. Someone called from the wrong side of the red recording light, using Eve's voice and saying exactly what a real person would say: that they were hurt, that the door was stuck, that Iris was leaving them there. Iris kept one hand against the wall and repeated the rule under their breath. That was when the recording booth moved close enough to cast a shadow where no light source existed.

The escape depended on a small human detail the thing had not learned. Iris left a false instruction where the pattern would find it, then moved without looking back to see whether it obeyed. For the first time, the event hesitated. Normal sound returned in pieces: a motor settling, air in a vent, rain or insects beyond glass, one distant vehicle moving through the real world. Iris reached the exit with the evidence still recording.

The recording is the reason the incident was not dismissed completely. In the final minute, Iris can be heard breathing hard and refusing to answer three separate times. After the third refusal, the microphone captures headphone hiss, booth glass tapping, and a second voice under the waveform, followed by a second voice saying Iris's name with the careful pronunciation of someone reading it from a file. Then the camera tilts down, though nobody admits touching it, and shows a thin line of wet dust leading away.

Morning did not make the case easier to explain. The report contained photographs, timestamps, and one damaged file, but no version of events that satisfied everyone. Officials preferred trespassers, stress, weather, faulty equipment, or sleep deprivation. The people who reviewed the evidence were less confident. In the final usable frame, Iris was visible leaving the red recording light, while another version of Iris remained beside the recording booth, perfectly still, as if waiting to be noticed again.

That is why the warning survived in practical language instead of superstition. No one close to the incident tells people to believe in ghosts. They tell them to respect the first impossible detail. They tell them not to test the rule for proof. And whenever headphone hiss, booth glass tapping, and a second voice under the waveform returns in a place it should not be, they stop speaking until it passes.

Iris never recorded silence again because silence had learned to answer.
