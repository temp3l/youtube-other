# Narration Script

The telescope moved by itself and focused on a point in darkness where something blinked.

Dr. Elise Carr was an observatory systems astronomer working in a mountain observatory closed to the public during a meteor shower. The night should have been simple: verify tracking coordinates, check the dome slit, and leave one clean record for morning. At 11:36 p.m., Elise noticed the telescope was wrong in a way that did not need belief. It did not begin with a scream. It began with a wrong timestamp, a shadow where no shadow belonged, and cold air moving at ankle height.

Elise did what a careful person does first. A photo was taken. The time was written down. The nearest exit was kept open. Then Elise tried to force the ordinary explanation to fit. Bad wiring, old pipes, a delayed feed, a prank, a corrupt file—each theory lasted only until the telescope changed again. The sound under it was unmistakable: telescope motors, cold wind over the dome, and radio pings returning too quickly. The sound did not grow louder. It grew more accurate.

Mina, the only other person reachable at that hour, told Elise to stop testing it. That warning landed harder than panic, because Mina sounded tired, like someone repeating a rule learned too late. “Listen to me,” Mina said. “If the telescope tracks an object without coordinates, close the dome immediately.” Elise laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear sometimes needs a smaller shape before the mind will hold it.

The second verification removed any comfort left in doubt. Tracking coordinates showed the event starting before Elise arrived. One image showed the dome slit empty. The next showed the telescope waiting in the exact spot Elise would later need to cross. The detail felt staged, but not for an audience. It felt staged for one decision. Whatever was present did not need to overpower anyone. It only needed a reasonable person to choose wrong.

In the archive, Elise found a complaint from 1989 with the same wording crossed out in blue pencil. The complainant had not asked for money. He had asked for his name to be removed from every copy. That made the night feel less like an accident and more like a procedure. The event had a boundary. It had a timing. It reacted when named, measured, opened, watched, bought, counted, answered, or followed. The rule was not folklore. It was the surviving edge of somebody else's failure.

At 1:48 a.m., the situation became personal. Elise's phone lit without ringing. The preview contained a sentence Elise had said earlier in the night, but the sender was listed as Elise. When the message opened, there was no text, only a short audio file. In it, Elise's own voice whispered, “I already did what you are thinking about doing.” Then came telescope motors, cold wind over the dome, and radio pings returning too quickly, so faint it could have been inside the speaker.

The next call came from the automated service line. The voice used correct names, correct addresses, and correct procedure. It said the danger was over, help had arrived, and the safest thing now was to interact with the telescope exactly once. That was the clever part. The instruction sounded like rescue, but it required the one action the rule had forbidden. Elise asked the caller to repeat the case number. The line went quiet long enough for the breathing on the other end to stop pretending.

Elise made a plan that would look sensible in a police report. Stop interacting with the telescope. Preserve the evidence. Move toward a public exit. Do not improvise. Do not answer. Do not touch the thing that wanted to be confirmed. For almost three minutes, it worked. The route out became ordinary again. The silence was so complete that it felt like permission to breathe.

Then the threat used the one voice that made caution feel cruel. Someone called from the wrong side of the dome slit, using Mina's voice and saying exactly what a real person would say: that they were hurt, that the door was stuck, that Elise was leaving them there. Elise kept one hand against the wall and repeated the rule under their breath. That was when the telescope moved close enough to cast a shadow where no light source existed.

The escape depended on a small human detail the thing had not learned. Elise left a false instruction where the pattern would find it, then moved without looking back to see whether it obeyed. For the first time, the event hesitated. Normal sound returned in pieces: a motor settling, air in a vent, rain or insects beyond glass, one distant vehicle moving through the real world. Elise reached the exit with the evidence still recording.

The recording is the reason the incident was not dismissed completely. In the final minute, Elise can be heard breathing hard and refusing to answer three separate times. After the third refusal, the microphone captures telescope motors, cold wind over the dome, and radio pings returning too quickly, followed by a second voice saying Elise's name with the careful pronunciation of someone reading it from a file. Then the camera tilts down, though nobody admits touching it, and shows a thin line of wet dust leading away.

Morning did not make the case easier to explain. The report contained photographs, timestamps, and one damaged file, but no version of events that satisfied everyone. Officials preferred trespassers, stress, weather, faulty equipment, or sleep deprivation. The people who reviewed the evidence were less confident. In the final usable frame, Elise was visible leaving the dome slit, while another version of Elise remained beside the telescope, perfectly still, as if waiting to be noticed again.

That is why the warning survived in practical language instead of superstition. No one close to the incident tells people to believe in ghosts. They tell them to respect the first impossible detail. They tell them not to test the rule for proof. And whenever telescope motors, cold wind over the dome, and radio pings returning too quickly returns in a place it should not be, they stop speaking until it passes.

The universe had noticed the observatory noticing.
