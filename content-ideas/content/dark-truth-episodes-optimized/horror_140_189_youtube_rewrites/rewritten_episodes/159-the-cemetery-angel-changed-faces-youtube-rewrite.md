# Narration Script

The cemetery angel had no face on Monday. By Friday, it was wearing Claire's.

Claire Wynn was a stone conservator working in a hillside cemetery overlooking an industrial town. The night should have been simple: verify restoration notes, check the western path, and leave one clean record for morning. At 12:33 a.m., Claire noticed the cemetery angel was wrong in a way that did not need belief. It did not begin with a scream. It began with a wrong timestamp, a shadow where no shadow belonged, and distant traffic thinning into silence.

Claire did what a careful person does first. A photo was taken. The time was written down. The nearest exit was kept open. Then Claire tried to force the ordinary explanation to fit. Bad wiring, old pipes, a delayed feed, a prank, a corrupt file—each theory lasted only until the cemetery angel changed again. The sound under it was unmistakable: stone dust brushing, wind through iron railings, and wings scraping granite. The sound did not grow louder. It grew more accurate.

Malik, the only other person reachable at that hour, told Claire to stop testing it. That warning landed harder than panic, because Malik sounded tired, like someone repeating a rule learned too late. “Listen to me,” Malik said. “If the angel statue has your face, leave before sunset.” Claire laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear sometimes needs a smaller shape before the mind will hold it.

The second verification removed any comfort left in doubt. Restoration notes showed the event starting before Claire arrived. One image showed the western path empty. The next showed the cemetery angel waiting in the exact spot Claire would later need to cross. The detail felt staged, but not for an audience. It felt staged for one decision. Whatever was present did not need to overpower anyone. It only needed a reasonable person to choose wrong.

The story had happened before, but every older account stopped at the same practical instruction: If the angel statue has your face, leave before sunset. That made the night feel less like an accident and more like a procedure. The event had a boundary. It had a timing. It reacted when named, measured, opened, watched, bought, counted, answered, or followed. The rule was not folklore. It was the surviving edge of somebody else's failure.

At 4:09 a.m., the situation became personal. Claire's phone lit without ringing. The preview contained a sentence Claire had said earlier in the night, but the sender was listed as Claire. When the message opened, there was no text, only a short audio file. In it, Claire's own voice whispered, “I already did what you are thinking about doing.” Then came stone dust brushing, wind through iron railings, and wings scraping granite, so faint it could have been inside the speaker.

The next call came from the old emergency contact. The voice used correct names, correct addresses, and correct procedure. It said the danger was over, help had arrived, and the safest thing now was to interact with the cemetery angel exactly once. That was the clever part. The instruction sounded like rescue, but it required the one action the rule had forbidden. Claire asked the caller to repeat the case number. The line went quiet long enough for the breathing on the other end to stop pretending.

Claire made a plan that would look sensible in a police report. Stop interacting with the cemetery angel. Preserve the evidence. Move toward a public exit. Do not improvise. Do not answer. Do not touch the thing that wanted to be confirmed. For almost three minutes, it worked. The route out became ordinary again. The silence was so complete that it felt like permission to breathe.

Then the threat used the one voice that made caution feel cruel. Someone called from the wrong side of the western path, using Malik's voice and saying exactly what a real person would say: that they were hurt, that the door was stuck, that Claire was leaving them there. Claire kept one hand against the wall and repeated the rule under their breath. That was when the cemetery angel moved close enough to cast a shadow where no light source existed.

The escape depended on a small human detail the thing had not learned. Claire left a false instruction where the pattern would find it, then moved without looking back to see whether it obeyed. For the first time, the event hesitated. Normal sound returned in pieces: a motor settling, air in a vent, rain or insects beyond glass, one distant vehicle moving through the real world. Claire reached the exit with the evidence still recording.

The recording is the reason the incident was not dismissed completely. In the final minute, Claire can be heard breathing hard and refusing to answer three separate times. After the third refusal, the microphone captures stone dust brushing, wind through iron railings, and wings scraping granite, followed by a second voice saying Claire's name with the careful pronunciation of someone reading it from a file. Then the camera tilts down, though nobody admits touching it, and shows a thin line of wet dust leading away.

Morning did not make the case easier to explain. The report contained photographs, timestamps, and one damaged file, but no version of events that satisfied everyone. Officials preferred trespassers, stress, weather, faulty equipment, or sleep deprivation. The people who reviewed the evidence were less confident. In the final usable frame, Claire was visible leaving the western path, while another version of Claire remained beside the cemetery angel, perfectly still, as if waiting to be noticed again.

That is why the warning survived in practical language instead of superstition. No one close to the incident tells people to believe in ghosts. They tell them to respect the first impossible detail. They tell them not to test the rule for proof. And whenever stone dust brushing, wind through iron railings, and wings scraping granite returns in a place it should not be, they stop speaking until it passes.

The angel had stopped changing faces because it had finally chosen one.
