# Narration Script

Daniel unlocked the condemned school at midnight and found a classroom full of adults sitting perfectly still.

Daniel Cross was an adult education teacher working in a condemned school being converted into apartments. The night should have been simple: verify attendance sheet, check the chalkboard, and leave one clean record for morning. At 12:55 a.m., Daniel noticed room 204 was wrong in a way that did not need belief. It did not begin with a scream. It began with a wrong timestamp, a shadow where no shadow belonged, and rain ticking against glass.

Daniel did what a careful person does first. A photo was taken. The time was written down. The nearest exit was kept open. Then Daniel tried to force the ordinary explanation to fit. Bad wiring, old pipes, a delayed feed, a prank, a corrupt file—each theory lasted only until room 204 changed again. The sound under it was unmistakable: chalk scraping, a school bell muffled by dust, and desks dragging across wooden floorboards. The sound did not grow louder. It grew more accurate.

Kim, the only other person reachable at that hour, told Daniel to stop testing it. That warning landed harder than panic, because Kim sounded tired, like someone repeating a rule learned too late. “Listen to me,” Kim said. “If the class is already seated, do not write your name on the board.” Daniel laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear sometimes needs a smaller shape before the mind will hold it.

The second verification removed any comfort left in doubt. Attendance sheet showed the event starting before Daniel arrived. One image showed the chalkboard empty. The next showed room 204 waiting in the exact spot Daniel would later need to cross. The detail felt staged, but not for an audience. It felt staged for one decision. Whatever was present did not need to overpower anyone. It only needed a reasonable person to choose wrong.

The oldest matching record was not filed as a haunting. It was filed as damaged equipment, missing time, and one witness who refused to give a second statement. That made the night feel less like an accident and more like a procedure. The event had a boundary. It had a timing. It reacted when named, measured, opened, watched, bought, counted, answered, or followed. The rule was not folklore. It was the surviving edge of somebody else's failure.

At 2:35 a.m., the situation became personal. Daniel's phone lit without ringing. The preview contained a sentence Daniel had said earlier in the night, but the sender was listed as Daniel. When the message opened, there was no text, only a short audio file. In it, Daniel's own voice whispered, “I already did what you are thinking about doing.” Then came chalk scraping, a school bell muffled by dust, and desks dragging across wooden floorboards, so faint it could have been inside the speaker.

The next call came from the police dispatcher. The voice used correct names, correct addresses, and correct procedure. It said the danger was over, help had arrived, and the safest thing now was to interact with room 204 exactly once. That was the clever part. The instruction sounded like rescue, but it required the one action the rule had forbidden. Daniel asked the caller to repeat the case number. The line went quiet long enough for the breathing on the other end to stop pretending.

Daniel made a plan that would look sensible in a police report. Stop interacting with room 204. Preserve the evidence. Move toward a public exit. Do not improvise. Do not answer. Do not touch the thing that wanted to be confirmed. For almost three minutes, it worked. The route out became ordinary again. The silence was so complete that it felt like permission to breathe.

Then the threat used the one voice that made caution feel cruel. Someone called from the wrong side of the chalkboard, using Kim's voice and saying exactly what a real person would say: that they were hurt, that the door was stuck, that Daniel was leaving them there. Daniel kept one hand against the wall and repeated the rule under their breath. That was when room 204 moved close enough to cast a shadow where no light source existed.

The escape depended on a small human detail the thing had not learned. Daniel left a false instruction where the pattern would find it, then moved without looking back to see whether it obeyed. For the first time, the event hesitated. Normal sound returned in pieces: a motor settling, air in a vent, rain or insects beyond glass, one distant vehicle moving through the real world. Daniel reached the exit with the evidence still recording.

The recording is the reason the incident was not dismissed completely. In the final minute, Daniel can be heard breathing hard and refusing to answer three separate times. After the third refusal, the microphone captures chalk scraping, a school bell muffled by dust, and desks dragging across wooden floorboards, followed by a second voice saying Daniel's name with the careful pronunciation of someone reading it from a file. Then the camera tilts down, though nobody admits touching it, and shows a thin line of wet dust leading away.

Morning did not make the case easier to explain. The report contained photographs, timestamps, and one damaged file, but no version of events that satisfied everyone. Officials preferred trespassers, stress, weather, faulty equipment, or sleep deprivation. The people who reviewed the evidence were less confident. In the final usable frame, Daniel was visible leaving the chalkboard, while another version of Daniel remained beside room 204, perfectly still, as if waiting to be noticed again.

That is why the warning survived in practical language instead of superstition. No one close to the incident tells people to believe in ghosts. They tell them to respect the first impossible detail. They tell them not to test the rule for proof. And whenever chalk scraping, a school bell muffled by dust, and desks dragging across wooden floorboards returns in a place it should not be, they stop speaking until it passes.

The lesson began again when he picked up the chalk.
