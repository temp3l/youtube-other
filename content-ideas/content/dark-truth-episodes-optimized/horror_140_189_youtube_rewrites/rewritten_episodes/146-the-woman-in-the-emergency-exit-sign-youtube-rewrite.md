# Narration Script

The green woman inside the emergency exit sign turned her head and looked down at Rhea.

Rhea Stone was a cinema projection technician working in an old six-screen cinema scheduled to close forever. The night should have been simple: verify projection-room breaker panel, check the side corridor, and leave one clean record for morning. At 11:02 p.m., Rhea noticed the emergency exit sign was wrong in a way that did not need belief. It did not begin with a scream. It began with a wrong timestamp, a shadow where no shadow belonged, and wind rubbing the walls.

Rhea did what a careful person does first. A photo was taken. The time was written down. The nearest exit was kept open. Then Rhea tried to force the ordinary explanation to fit. Bad wiring, old pipes, a delayed feed, a prank, a corrupt file—each theory lasted only until the emergency exit sign changed again. The sound under it was unmistakable: projector rattle, exit sign buzz, and a woman's fingernails tapping plastic. The sound did not grow louder. It grew more accurate.

Lotte, the only other person reachable at that hour, told Rhea to stop testing it. That warning landed harder than panic, because Lotte sounded tired, like someone repeating a rule learned too late. “Listen to me,” Lotte said. “If the figure in the exit sign points the wrong way, do not follow it.” Rhea laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear sometimes needs a smaller shape before the mind will hold it.

The second verification removed any comfort left in doubt. Projection-room breaker panel showed the event starting before Rhea arrived. One image showed the side corridor empty. The next showed the emergency exit sign waiting in the exact spot Rhea would later need to cross. The detail felt staged, but not for an audience. It felt staged for one decision. Whatever was present did not need to overpower anyone. It only needed a reasonable person to choose wrong.

Lotte found the first matching report in a scanned folder labeled maintenance, although the handwriting on the last page looked fresh. That made the night feel less like an accident and more like a procedure. The event had a boundary. It had a timing. It reacted when named, measured, opened, watched, bought, counted, answered, or followed. The rule was not folklore. It was the surviving edge of somebody else's failure.

At 3:46 a.m., the situation became personal. Rhea's phone lit without ringing. The preview contained a sentence Rhea had said earlier in the night, but the sender was listed as Rhea. When the message opened, there was no text, only a short audio file. In it, Rhea's own voice whispered, “I already did what you are thinking about doing.” Then came projector rattle, exit sign buzz, and a woman's fingernails tapping plastic, so faint it could have been inside the speaker.

The next call came from the night supervisor. The voice used correct names, correct addresses, and correct procedure. It said the danger was over, help had arrived, and the safest thing now was to interact with the emergency exit sign exactly once. That was the clever part. The instruction sounded like rescue, but it required the one action the rule had forbidden. Rhea asked the caller to repeat the case number. The line went quiet long enough for the breathing on the other end to stop pretending.

Rhea made a plan that would look sensible in a police report. Stop interacting with the emergency exit sign. Preserve the evidence. Move toward a public exit. Do not improvise. Do not answer. Do not touch the thing that wanted to be confirmed. For almost three minutes, it worked. The route out became ordinary again. The silence was so complete that it felt like permission to breathe.

Then the threat used the one voice that made caution feel cruel. Someone called from the wrong side of the side corridor, using Lotte's voice and saying exactly what a real person would say: that they were hurt, that the door was stuck, that Rhea was leaving them there. Rhea kept one hand against the wall and repeated the rule under their breath. That was when the emergency exit sign moved close enough to cast a shadow where no light source existed.

The escape depended on a small human detail the thing had not learned. Rhea left a false instruction where the pattern would find it, then moved without looking back to see whether it obeyed. For the first time, the event hesitated. Normal sound returned in pieces: a motor settling, air in a vent, rain or insects beyond glass, one distant vehicle moving through the real world. Rhea reached the exit with the evidence still recording.

The recording is the reason the incident was not dismissed completely. In the final minute, Rhea can be heard breathing hard and refusing to answer three separate times. After the third refusal, the microphone captures projector rattle, exit sign buzz, and a woman's fingernails tapping plastic, followed by a second voice saying Rhea's name with the careful pronunciation of someone reading it from a file. Then the camera tilts down, though nobody admits touching it, and shows a thin line of wet dust leading away.

Morning did not make the case easier to explain. The report contained photographs, timestamps, and one damaged file, but no version of events that satisfied everyone. Officials preferred trespassers, stress, weather, faulty equipment, or sleep deprivation. The people who reviewed the evidence were less confident. In the final usable frame, Rhea was visible leaving the side corridor, while another version of Rhea remained beside the emergency exit sign, perfectly still, as if waiting to be noticed again.

That is why the warning survived in practical language instead of superstition. No one close to the incident tells people to believe in ghosts. They tell them to respect the first impossible detail. They tell them not to test the rule for proof. And whenever projector rattle, exit sign buzz, and a woman's fingernails tapping plastic returns in a place it should not be, they stop speaking until it passes.

Rhea followed the only exit left, and the sign closed behind her.
