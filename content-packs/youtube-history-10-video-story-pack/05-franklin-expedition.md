# The Mystery of the Franklin Expedition: Lost in the Arctic

## Core hook

> Two reinforced ships, 129 men, and three years of provisions entered the Arctic in 1845. None returned—but Inuit testimony and underwater archaeology have reconstructed part of their final journey.

## Documentary story / narration

In May 1845, two Royal Navy ships sailed from Britain to search for the Northwest Passage. HMS Erebus and HMS Terror were strong, experienced polar vessels. They carried steam engines, reinforced hulls, scientific instruments, preserved food, and 129 officers and men under Sir John Franklin.

They were seen by whaling ships in Baffin Bay that summer. Then they vanished.

For years, Britain sent search expeditions into the Arctic. They found graves, abandoned equipment, human remains, and one written message. No survivor returned to explain what happened. The Franklin Expedition became a national obsession—and one of the great mysteries of exploration history.

Britain’s interest in the Northwest Passage was strategic, scientific, and symbolic. A navigable route through the Arctic might connect the Atlantic and Pacific. Naval officers hoped to complete the map of the northern coast of North America. Success promised prestige in an age that celebrated imperial exploration.

Franklin was already a famous Arctic veteran. Erebus and Terror had recently returned from James Clark Ross’s Antarctic expedition. Their hulls were strengthened for ice. Auxiliary steam engines could drive propellers when wind failed. Heating systems, preserved provisions, and libraries suggested that the expedition was prepared for several winters.

But preparation did not eliminate uncertainty. Arctic charts were incomplete. Sea ice could trap ships for years. Magnetic compasses behaved unpredictably near the pole. Scurvy, tuberculosis, pneumonia, frostbite, and accidents threatened crews far from help. Food preservation technology was improving but imperfect.

The first confirmed traces appeared on Beechey Island. Searchers found the remains of the expedition’s winter camp from 1845 to 1846 and the graves of three sailors: John Torrington, John Hartnell, and William Braine. The ships had wintered there before sailing south through Peel Sound.

In September 1846, Erebus and Terror became trapped in ice off King William Island. They remained trapped through 1847. Franklin died on June 11 of that year, according to the only major written record later recovered.

That record is known as the Victory Point note. It was placed in a stone cairn. The original message, written in 1847, reported that all was well. A second message added in April 1848 transformed it into a disaster report. The ships had been trapped for more than a year and a half. Franklin and twenty-three others were dead. The 105 survivors, led by Francis Crozier and James Fitzjames, had abandoned the ships on April 22 and planned to march toward the Back River on the Canadian mainland.

The decision to leave the ships is central to the mystery. A ship offered shelter, stores, tools, and fuel. But if the ice never released it, remaining aboard could mean slow starvation. The crews may have believed a march south offered the only chance of reaching trading routes or settlements.

They hauled heavy boats mounted on sledges. Searchers later found one of these boats containing equipment that seemed strange for men fighting for survival: books, silverware, clothing, and other objects. Victorians interpreted this as evidence of irrational discipline or poor leadership. But the boat may have been used at different stages, and the objects could have been selected for trade, repair, status, or practical reasons not obvious to later observers.

Human remains found along King William Island and farther south show that groups traveled considerable distances. Cut marks on some bones provide evidence that at least some survivors resorted to cannibalism. In Victorian Britain, reports of cannibalism were treated as an attack on the honor of the Royal Navy. The messenger was often blamed.

That messenger was largely Inuit testimony. Inuit witnesses described encounters with starving white men, abandoned camps, bodies, objects, and ships. Some accounts suggested that at least one ship was still afloat after the official abandonment. European searchers recorded these stories unevenly and sometimes dismissed details that did not fit their assumptions.

Modern archaeology has repeatedly shown the value of Inuit knowledge. In 2014, a Canadian search located HMS Erebus. In 2016, HMS Terror was found in remarkably preserved condition in Terror Bay. Inuit oral histories were instrumental in directing attention toward the areas where the wrecks were discovered.

The locations created new questions. The wrecks were not simply left where the Victory Point note said the ships had been trapped. At least one ship moved. It may have drifted with the ice, or survivors may have returned, remanned it, and attempted to sail. The wreck of Terror, with hatches closed and much of the interior preserved, suggests a more complicated final phase than a single abandonment followed by a march.

Why did everyone die? There is no single confirmed cause. Scurvy likely weakened some men, although evidence varies. Tuberculosis and respiratory disease were present. Lead exposure from soldered food tins and the ships’ water systems has been investigated, but whether it played a decisive role remains debated. Malnutrition, exhaustion, injuries, cold, infection, and the immense difficulty of hauling loads across broken sea ice likely compounded one another.

Leadership decisions also mattered. The expedition followed naval routines designed for discipline and order, but the Arctic environment punished inflexibility. Yet it is easy to judge with hindsight. The officers did not know whether rescue was approaching, whether the ice would break, or which route offered food and safety.

The Franklin story was long framed as heroic British explorers defeated by an empty wilderness. The Arctic was not empty. Inuit communities possessed generations of environmental knowledge, travel techniques, clothing systems, hunting skills, and oral memory. The tragedy is also a story of Europeans failing to recognize expertise that could have changed how the expedition operated—and later failing to trust testimony that described its fate.

The wrecks have not solved every question. They have changed the shape of the mystery. Artifacts, ship layouts, sediment, and future archaeological work may reveal whether survivors returned aboard, how the ships moved, and what happened during the final months.

The material evidence also complicates the idea that canned food alone doomed the expedition. Some tins were poorly soldered, and early tests found elevated lead in remains. Later research showed that nineteenth-century people could accumulate lead from many sources over a lifetime. Lead may have impaired some crew members, but current evidence does not justify treating poisoning as the single explanation. The same caution applies to botulism and other proposed causes.

Archaeology is unusually powerful here because cold water preserved the wrecks. Cabins, plates, instruments, storage areas, and personal objects remain in context. Yet recovery must be slow: once artifacts leave the water, conservation can take years. The site is also inseparable from Inuit heritage and is jointly managed, shifting the story away from a purely British tale of exploration.

Franklin’s expedition did not disappear without evidence. Its history survived in a note under stones, in scattered remains, in objects carried across ice, in ships on the seabed, and in stories preserved by Inuit witnesses. The mystery endured partly because the people searching for answers did not always know which evidence to believe.
