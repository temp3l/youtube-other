# The Cuban Missile Crisis: Thirteen Days Near Nuclear War

## Core hook

> The Cuban Missile Crisis was not a clean chess match controlled from Washington and Moscow. Nuclear war was avoided despite miscommunication, accidents, unauthorized pressure, and decisions made by people at sea, in aircraft, and inside command rooms.

## Documentary story / narration

On October 16, 1962, President John F. Kennedy was shown photographs taken by an American U-2 reconnaissance aircraft. Analysts had identified Soviet medium-range ballistic missile sites under construction in Cuba.

Once operational, the missiles could strike much of the United States with nuclear warheads. Kennedy now faced a secret crisis. If he acted too slowly, the sites might become ready. If he attacked, the Soviet Union might retaliate in Berlin, Europe, or with nuclear weapons.

For thirteen days, officials in Washington and Moscow tried to influence one another while operating with incomplete information. The crisis is often remembered as a controlled contest between two leaders. In reality, it included accidents, conflicting signals, military momentum, local commanders, and events neither leader fully controlled.

The confrontation began before the missiles arrived. In 1959, Fidel Castro’s revolution overthrew the Batista dictatorship. Relations with the United States deteriorated. In April 1961, a U.S.-backed invasion by Cuban exiles failed at the Bay of Pigs. American covert operations continued, and Cuban leaders expected another invasion.

Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev saw an opportunity. Deploying nuclear missiles to Cuba could protect Castro’s government, alter the strategic balance, and respond to American nuclear missiles positioned near Soviet territory, including Jupiter missiles in Turkey. The operation was conducted secretly. Soviet troops and equipment traveled disguised aboard ships.

Kennedy formed the Executive Committee of the National Security Council, known as ExComm. Its members debated air strikes, invasion, diplomacy, and a naval blockade. Military leaders emphasized the danger of allowing the missiles to become operational. But an air strike might fail to destroy every missile and could resemble a surprise attack.

Kennedy chose a naval “quarantine.” The word blockade could imply an act of war. American ships would stop vessels carrying offensive military equipment to Cuba. At the same time, the United States prepared for air attacks and invasion.

On October 22, Kennedy announced the discovery publicly. He demanded the removal of the missiles and warned that any nuclear attack from Cuba would be treated as an attack by the Soviet Union. Around the world, military forces raised readiness levels. Families watched television and wondered whether cities might soon disappear.

The quarantine line became the first immediate test. Some Soviet ships changed course or stopped. Others continued. American officials tried to avoid a confrontation that could escalate from warning shots to war.

Communication was slow and ambiguous. Washington and Moscow exchanged formal messages, public statements, intelligence assessments, and back-channel contacts. Each side attempted to appear firm without closing every path to compromise.

On October 26, Khrushchev sent a message that appeared emotional and personal. He proposed removing the missiles if the United States pledged not to invade Cuba. The next day, a tougher public message added a demand: the United States should remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey.

The second proposal created a dilemma. Accepting publicly could make the United States appear to trade allied security under nuclear pressure. Rejecting it could eliminate the basis for peace. Kennedy’s advisers developed a strategy: respond publicly to the first message while privately assuring the Soviets that the Jupiter missiles would be removed later, provided the arrangement remained secret.

Then came October 27—later called Black Saturday.

A U-2 aircraft was shot down over Cuba, killing Major Rudolf Anderson. American military plans called for retaliation against such an attack. Kennedy chose not to strike immediately. He could not be certain that Khrushchev had ordered the shootdown, and retaliation might begin an unstoppable sequence.

On the same day, another U-2 accidentally strayed over Soviet territory near the Arctic. Soviet fighters rose to intercept it. American fighters carrying nuclear-armed air-to-air missiles were sent to protect it. A navigational error had created a second potential nuclear confrontation almost entirely separate from the crisis in Cuba.

At sea, American destroyers were using practice depth charges to signal Soviet submarines to surface. The Americans did not know that some of those submarines carried nuclear torpedoes.

Aboard submarine B-59, conditions were extreme. The vessel had been submerged, communications were limited, and the crew did not know whether war had already begun. American signaling explosions shook the submarine. Captain Valentin Savitsky considered using the nuclear torpedo.

Accounts of the exact decision process vary, but senior officer Vasili Arkhipov opposed escalation and supported surfacing. B-59 did not launch. A decision unknown to Kennedy and Khrushchev had become part of whether nuclear war began.

Cuban leaders were also not passive. Castro expected invasion and urged readiness. In a message to Khrushchev, he discussed the possibility of a Soviet nuclear response if the United States attacked. Khrushchev interpreted the situation as evidence that events were approaching catastrophe.

On October 28, Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be dismantled and removed under verification. The United States publicly pledged not to invade Cuba. Privately, the Jupiter missiles in Turkey were removed months later, though Washington insisted the withdrawal not appear to be a direct public trade.

The crisis ended, but not instantly. Soviet bombers and other equipment remained subjects of negotiation. Verification was incomplete. Cuba rejected on-site inspections. Both superpowers had to manage allies and public narratives.

The aftermath produced changes intended to reduce future risk. A direct communications link—the Washington–Moscow hotline—was established the following year. The United States and Soviet Union signed the Limited Nuclear Test Ban Treaty in 1963. Both governments studied the danger of escalation and misperception.

Yet the central lesson is not that wise leaders controlled every move. Kennedy and Khrushchev made crucial choices, including decisions to delay, reinterpret, and preserve private compromise. But war was also avoided because individuals in aircraft, submarines, ships, and command structures did not push every encounter to its violent conclusion.

The missiles in Cuba also affected the balance differently in political and military terms. The Soviet Union already possessed weapons capable of striking the United States, but it had fewer intercontinental systems than Washington. Missiles in Cuba reduced warning time and created a visible challenge close to American territory. For Khrushchev, the deployment promised strategic leverage at relatively low cost. For Kennedy, allowing it to stand threatened credibility with allies and domestic opponents.

Neither government fully respected Cuba’s sovereignty during the settlement. The Soviet Union agreed to remove weapons without Castro participating directly in the final bargain. The United States promised not to invade but continued hostility and economic pressure. From Havana, the crisis was not simply a superpower drama; it was an existential confrontation conducted around Cuba by states with much larger nuclear arsenals.

The Cuban Missile Crisis came close to nuclear war not only because the superpowers intended to threaten one another. It came close because military systems continued moving while political leaders searched for an exit. The world survived through judgment, restraint, secrecy, and luck—and no one involved knew how much luck was required until years later.
