Two customers buy the same pair of earrings. One needs a gift tonight. The other wants a small reward after a terrible week. Same product, completely different reason to care. Now imagine your customer definition says, “Women, 25 to 45, interested in fashion.” What should your headline say? Which emotion should the ad use? Where is the urgency? The demographics do not answer the decision. That is why “anyone who could buy” is not a customer strategy — and a decorative persona is not much better. Build the definition around three things instead: need, context, signal. What is the person trying to achieve, feel, or avoid? When does that need become important enough to act? What behavior shows the interest is real? Then force the persona to change something concrete. If nothing changes, it is probably marketing decoration. Specificity is about making the message recognizable to someone at the moment it matters.

A useful customer definition should change the creative before you publish. The problem in the hook, the example in the middle, the objection you answer, and the next step can all change when the buying context changes. That is what makes specificity operational.

The test is practical, not demographic: a better customer definition should change something you actually do.

If your persona does not change at least one concrete decision, make it more specific.
