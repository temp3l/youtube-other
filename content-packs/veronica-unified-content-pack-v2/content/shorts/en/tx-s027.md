If an offer is not selling, making it cheaper does not automatically make it better.

A weak offer at twenty percent off is still a weak offer. It is just cheaper.

Discounts work best when the buyer already understands the value and needs a reason to act now. But if people do not understand who the offer is for, what problem it solves, what changes after they buy, or why they should trust you, the discount solves none of those problems.

Worse, repeated discounts can train customers to wait. If the “real” price appears every few weeks, the full price stops feeling real.

Before cutting the price, audit the offer itself.

Is the promise clear? Is the outcome specific? Does the package fit the buyer’s actual problem? Is the mechanism believable? Is there enough proof? Is the next step easy to understand?

Sometimes the best improvement is not another bonus either. More material can make an already confusing offer feel heavier.

Make the value easier to see before you make the price smaller.

A strong discount creates urgency around an offer people already want.

It should not be life support for an offer they never understood.

Try one test before the next promotion: ask a potential buyer to explain the offer back to you. If they cannot describe the result and why it matters, the problem is not the coupon code.
