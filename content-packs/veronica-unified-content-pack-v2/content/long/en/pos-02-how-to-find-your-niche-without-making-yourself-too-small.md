Trying to sell to everyone feels safer. In practice, it often makes you easier to ignore.

When your message has to fit every customer, it becomes broad enough that nobody immediately thinks, “This is for me.” A niche fixes that by making your relevance obvious.

The useful question is not how small you can make your market. It is how precisely you can connect a market, a problem, and a solution.

Start with the market.

You need information about the people you want to serve. The better you understand them, the more precise your positioning can become.

Who are they?

What situation are they in?

What do they care about?

What frustrates them?

What do they already know?

What have they already tried?

The point is not to create an abstract customer profile and then forget it. The point is to understand the context in which a buying decision actually happens.

This is where the idea of a niche becomes useful.

A niche is not a prison.

It is a focusing mechanism.

And sometimes a sub-niche can be even more useful because specificity makes communication more effective.

Imagine two professionals.

The first says, “I help businesses with marketing.”

The second says, “I help independent service professionals turn their expertise into a clear offer people can understand.”

The second person has said less, but communicated more.

You immediately have a better idea of who the offer is for.

That does not mean this person is forbidden from helping anybody else. It means the market has a clear way to understand the offer.

The fear behind niche positioning usually sounds like this: if I become too specific, I will lose all the people outside that group.

But broad markets contain different people with different needs, emotions, motivations, and expectations. Trying to address all of them with one generic message often produces something that feels relevant to none of them.

Precision is not about making your business artificially tiny.

It is about making your message intelligible.

Once you understand the market, move to the second element: the problem.

This is crucial because people do not always arrive already knowing the solution they need.

If they already understood the problem perfectly, knew the correct solution, and knew exactly how to apply it, they might not need you.

What they do know is what they are experiencing.

They know the frustration.

They know what is not working.

They know the consequence.

They know the feeling that something needs to change.

So your communication has to explain the problem in language that makes the person recognize their own situation.

This is why positioning is not just a label.

It is also an act of interpretation.

You are showing the customer that you understand what is happening to them.

When someone hears a clear description of a problem they have been struggling to explain, something important happens. They start paying attention.

Not because you have announced your credentials.

Because you have demonstrated understanding.

Now we reach the third element: the solution.

Your solution is where your expertise becomes concrete.

It can be a service, product, consultation, course, process, system, shop, or experience. But it should connect logically to the problem you have described.

This connection sounds obvious, yet many offers reverse the order.

They begin by explaining features.

They begin with a method.

They begin with a package name.

They begin with the thing they want to sell.

But the customer is still thinking about the problem.

A better sequence is simple.

First, understand the market.

Second, make the problem visible.

Third, introduce the solution as the logical response to that problem.

This also helps you differentiate.

If you understand a specific group better, describe their problem more precisely, and provide a solution adapted to that context, your positioning becomes harder to confuse with a generic competitor.

And this is where niche positioning becomes valuable.

You are not saying, “Nobody else is allowed to buy from me.”

You are saying, “These are the people I understand deeply enough to speak to clearly.”

There is another useful consequence.

A niche gives your content direction.

Instead of constantly asking, “What should I post today?”, you can work from the problems, questions, fears, and decisions that repeatedly appear inside that market.

Your content becomes easier to plan because your positioning gives you boundaries.

Those boundaries are productive.

They help people understand what you stand for.

They help you decide what not to talk about.

They help your audience connect separate pieces of content into one recognizable body of expertise.

So how do you know whether your niche is useful?

Try this test.

Can you describe the people you serve without saying “everyone”?

Can you describe a problem they recognize?

Can you explain why that problem matters to them?

Can you connect your solution directly to it?

And can someone outside your business understand the whole thing without needing a ten-minute explanation?

If the answer is yes, your positioning is becoming clearer.

If the answer is no, the solution may not be to add more services, more audiences, and more messages.

You may need to become more precise.

Start with the market.

Find the group you understand.

Listen to the problem.

Describe it clearly.

Then build the solution around that understanding.

Because a niche does not make you small.

A good niche makes you visible.

Of course, there is a second fear behind niching: what if the niche really is too small?

That is a legitimate business question, and it should be answered with evidence rather than anxiety.

A useful niche needs more than a clear description. It needs enough people with a meaningful problem, enough willingness to pay for a solution, and a realistic way for you to reach them.

So do not confuse specificity in positioning with blindness in market selection.

You can be extremely specific about a market that has no urgency, no budget, or no accessible demand. That is not good positioning. It is simply a weak commercial opportunity described precisely.

The goal is to find the intersection between relevance and viability.

Imagine that you help photographers. That is a market. But “photographers” may still contain wedding photographers, fashion photographers, product photographers, beginners, agencies, freelancers, and studio owners with completely different problems.

Now imagine you notice a repeated problem among established wedding photographers: they are booked, but their packages are confusing and they struggle to raise prices without losing inquiries.

Suddenly the position becomes much more useful.

You are not serving a smaller group merely for the sake of being narrow. You have identified a group, a painful decision, and an outcome with economic value.

That gives you better language. It gives you better content. It gives you better examples. It can even give you a better offer because the offer can be designed around a recognizable situation instead of a generic promise.

And a niche does not have to be permanent.

Think of it as a wedge into the market.

You become known for solving one problem for one recognizable group. You collect results. You learn the language. You build proof. Then, if the business supports it, you can expand into adjacent problems or adjacent groups without losing the original clarity.

This is much easier than trying to launch with five audiences and twelve problems at once.

There is also a simple warning sign that your niche is becoming artificially restrictive: you find yourself excluding good customers who have essentially the same problem for no strategic reason.

If two groups buy for the same reason, respond to the same language, and need the same solution, you may not need to separate them just to make the positioning sound narrower.

The niche should improve recognition, not force complexity into the business.

So test your niche in the real market. Listen to sales conversations. Read questions people ask. Notice which messages get an immediate “that is exactly my problem.” Pay attention to who buys, why they buy, and which words they use when they explain the problem back to you.

That evidence is more useful than choosing a niche because it sounds fashionable or because somebody told you that specificity is always better.

Good positioning is precise, but it is also commercially alive.

There is a practical way to make this even clearer.

Write three versions of your positioning, each more specific than the last.

Start broad.

Then narrow the audience.

Then narrow the problem.

Read all three versions as if you were the customer.

Which one makes it easiest to say, “This is for me”?

That is usually the level at which positioning begins to work.

You can always expand later. In fact, a strong niche can become the foundation from which you expand because people already understand your original expertise. What is difficult is doing the reverse: starting so broad that nobody knows why they should remember you.

Specificity gives you a foothold.

Once the market knows what you are the person for, you have something solid to build on.
