Try this positioning test. Ask yourself what you want to be known for. Then ask a client or colleague to describe what they think you are known for, without showing them your answer. If the answers match, your positioning is clear. If they do not, that gap is valuable information.

Ask three to five people separately and write down their exact words. Do not ask whether they like your brand. Ask, “What would you recommend me for?” and “What problem would make you think of my name?”

Now compare the answers. If one person says strategy, another says social media, another says coaching, and a fourth cannot answer, you have learned something important. Your public signals may be individually good, but together they are not creating one strong association.

Then do the reverse test. Look at your last ten public signals: posts, talks, offers, case studies, profile copy, collaborations. What expertise would a stranger infer from those alone? That is much closer to your actual position than what you intended to communicate.

That gap shows which signals must change next.

Positioning is not the message inside your head. It is the category your name occupies in other people’s memory. And when the right problem appears, memory matters because the person they remember is often the person they contact.
