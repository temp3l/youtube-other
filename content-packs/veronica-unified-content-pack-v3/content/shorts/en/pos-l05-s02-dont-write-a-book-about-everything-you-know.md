A consultant spends a year writing about marketing, mindset, sales, branding, leadership, and communication. The manuscript proves she knows a lot — and makes it hard to say what she is known for. That weakens a positioning book.

A stronger book usually does the opposite. Choose one reader, one important problem, and one professional territory you want associated with your name.

Use a one-sentence test before you write: “This book helps this kind of reader understand or solve this specific problem.” If that sentence needs five commas, the position is probably too broad.

Focus does not mean the book has to feel repetitive. You can go deep through stories, mistakes, methods, examples, objections, case studies, and different situations while keeping the same central promise.

Here is another test: imagine someone recommends the book six months after reading it. What sentence do you want them to say? “This is a great book about business” is weak. “Read this if you are a consultant whose expertise is difficult to explain” is much stronger.

That sentence tells you whether the book has a position.

A positioning book works when somebody can finish it and easily explain two things: why another person should read it, and why your name belongs with that subject. If they cannot do that, the book may contain knowledge without creating a position.
