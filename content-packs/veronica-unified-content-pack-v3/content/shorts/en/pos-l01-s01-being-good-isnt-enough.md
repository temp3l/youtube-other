Two consultants pitch for the same client. The first may be better: ten years of experience, excellent results, deep expertise. But her website says she does strategy, branding, sales, content, and growth. The second is known for solving one specific commercial problem and shows three clear examples of it. Who feels safer to hire?

That is the uncomfortable part of expertise: customers cannot inspect everything you know before they buy. They see your offer, content, website, what others say, and what they remember you for.

Being an expert and being perceived as an expert are two different problems.

Your work builds the expertise. Your positioning makes that expertise visible.

Talented people often assume quality should speak for itself. But quality usually speaks after someone has hired you. Positioning has to work before that decision. It gives the buyer enough clarity to think, “This person is relevant to my problem, and I can see why.”

So do not ask only, “Am I good enough?” Ask, “Could a stranger recognize what I am especially good at in five seconds?”

If the answer is no, the problem may not be your competence. It may be that your competence is hidden inside a message that looks like everyone else’s.

The goal is not to look impressive. The goal is to make real expertise easy to recognize.
