When sales slow down, more marketing is almost an automatic reaction. But if the problem is the product, more traffic does not fix it; it simply exposes the weakness to more people. Before buying more attention, separate a distribution problem from a value, demand, complexity, or format problem. That diagnosis is less comfortable, but it is often dramatically cheaper.

Post more.

Change the creative.

Run ads.

Rebuild the website.

But sometimes the problem is much further upstream.

Sometimes marketing is simply bringing more people to a product that is not working well enough.

Spending more to attract attention does not fix that problem.

It amplifies it.

So start with a harder question: have you actually validated what you are trying to sell?

Being good at something does not prove that a market is willing to pay for it in the form you designed.

You may love ceramics and be excellent at teaching it, and still need to understand what already exists, how alternatives are packaged, what people are paying for, and what they are trying to achieve.

Studying competitors does not mean copying them.

It means reading the market.

How is the product structured?

How broad is the range?

How is it delivered?

Which promises appear again and again?

What seems to be missing?

And be careful with another trap.

“No competitors” is not automatically good news.

You may have found an open space.

Or you may have found a market with almost no demand.

You need evidence to know which one it is.

Then comes the second issue: needs change.

A product can work perfectly in one context and become less relevant when the customer's life changes.

During lockdown periods, for example, some shops and services could not continue selling in exactly the same way.

That did not necessarily mean the underlying need had disappeared.

In some cases the need changed shape.

People spent more time at home.

They repaired things.

They looked for hands-on activities.

They wanted more autonomy and something meaningful to do with their own hands.

If you keep offering the old format while the need changes, you can conclude that “the market stopped buying.”

Maybe the market is buying something else.

Or maybe it wants the same solution delivered in a different form.

So do not ask only, “What product can I sell?”

Ask, “What practical or emotional job is the customer trying to do now?”

Sometimes that leads to kits.

Repair products.

Do-it-yourself versions.

Services turned into products.

Products turned into experiences.

The important thing is not to confuse the format with the need.

Then we reach another huge source of inefficiency: too much choice.

A restaurant with an enormous menu appears to offer more freedom.

But behind that apparent freedom are more ingredients to purchase, more inventory, more preparation, more waste, more training, and more opportunities for mistakes.

The customer can become more confused too.

More choice does not always create more conversion.

Sometimes it creates paralysis.

If there are fifty almost identical options in front of me, I have to work to understand which one is right.

If there are three clearly differentiated options, the decision can become much easier.

Simplifying the range can improve both the customer experience and the economics of the business.

Fewer variants.

Less inventory.

Fewer errors.

Fewer explanations.

More clarity.

The same principle applies to suppliers.

Optimization does not always mean finding the lowest unit price.

Imagine saving a few cents by buying from five different suppliers.

If you then spend hours coordinating orders, deliveries, invoices, exceptions, and problems, the apparent saving may cost much more in time and complexity.

The real cost is not only the number written on the invoice.

It includes the founder's time.

Coordination.

Risk.

Errors.

Fragmentation.

Sometimes paying a little more to one reliable supplier reduces the total cost of the system.

And when inventory is expensive or demand is uncertain, you can sometimes reduce risk before filling the warehouse.

Preorders are one example.

They can help you test whether demand exists before tying up capital in stock.

That does not mean selling something you cannot deliver.

It means collecting market signals before assuming all of the risk.

At this point the question changes completely.

It is no longer, “How do I promote this product better?”

It becomes:

Does this product still solve a real need?

Have I looked at what is happening in the market?

Am I interpreting competition correctly?

Has customer behavior changed?

Am I offering too many variants?

Is internal complexity eating the margin?

Am I optimizing unit price or the entire system?

Can I validate before investing more?

Sometimes the smartest move is not another campaign.

It is removing a product.

Combining two variants.

Changing the format.

Simplifying the process.

Reducing the number of suppliers.

Testing a different delivery model.

Here is a way to avoid spending months improving the wrong marketing. Take the most important product assumption and turn it into a small test. If you think people want a new premium version, do not immediately order a thousand units. Show the concept to real customers, ask which problem it solves, observe what they would choose instead of their current alternative, and when possible test a preorder or another real commitment. Compliments are free. A real choice contains much more information.

Or admitting that the thing you wanted to sell is not the thing the market wants to buy.

That can actually be good news.

If the problem is the product, you can work on the product.

If you keep calling it a marketing problem, you may spend time and money persuading more people to look at something that is not strong enough yet.

Before buying more attention, make sure the product deserves it.

You can turn this into a concrete diagnostic sequence.

First: does the need exist?

Not, “I love this idea.”

Not, “I am good at making it.”

Is there someone already trying to solve this problem?

Then study the competitors.

Competitors are not automatically bad news.

They may confirm that demand exists.

They show how the market currently presents the solution.

They reveal what customers consider normal.

And they can show where there is room for a meaningful difference.

The absence of competitors deserves the same skepticism.

You may have discovered a valuable gap.

Or you may have discovered something very few people want.

Validate it. Do not fall in love with the empty space.

Then ask whether the need has changed.

The lockdown example matters because people did not simply stop having needs.

Their conditions changed.

Their emotions changed.

What they were able to do changed.

Products connected to DIY, repair, or making things by hand could become more relevant because they matched the new context.

The lesson is not “sell kits during a crisis.”

The lesson is: do not confuse the old form of the need with the need itself.

Next, inspect the complexity you have accumulated.

A restaurant with a huge menu has to buy more ingredients, carry more stock, and support more operational combinations.

Meanwhile the customer has to choose between more alternatives.

What looks like freedom can become waste on one side and confusion on the other.

The same thing happens with products, packages, and services.

If you have thirty variants that do almost the same thing, ask which ones are actually contributing.

Sometimes reducing them to three clear choices improves the customer experience and the operation at the same time.

Then look at costs that do not appear immediately in the unit price.

Your time.

Coordination.

The number of suppliers.

Exceptions.

Small tasks that interrupt the day.

The cheapest supplier is not always the cheapest system if it requires more orders, more checking, and more attention.

Optimization means looking at the whole system.

If inventory is the main risk, test demand before buying everything you might eventually need.

A preorder is only one example.

A waitlist, a smaller pilot, or a structured customer conversation can also provide useful signals.

None of those is absolute proof.

But they are better than internal enthusiasm alone.

The purpose of validation is not to prove that you were right.

It is to discover early enough whether something needs to change.

Build a product audit around a few dimensions:

Real demand.

Competition.

Current customer need.

Number of variants.

Operational cost.

Time.

Suppliers.

Inventory risk.

Then decide what to remove, what to change, and what to test.

After every test, write down what you learned, not only whether you sold.

Which question kept appearing?

Which option created confusion?

Which part attracted interest?

Which assumption turned out to be wrong?

Validation becomes valuable when every attempt produces information for the next version.

This work may feel less exciting than launching a new campaign.

But if it improves what the customer finds after the click, it can have a much deeper effect.
