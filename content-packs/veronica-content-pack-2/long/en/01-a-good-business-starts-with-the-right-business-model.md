You can double your revenue and make your business more fragile at the same time. It happens when every extra sale brings almost as much cost, delivery work, support, and complexity with it. Before asking how to sell more, run the harder test: if orders doubled tomorrow, would the business actually become healthier — or just busier?

Because revenue does not automatically mean you have a good business model.

If one hundred euros comes in and ninety-five goes straight back out to produce, deliver, support, and sustain the sale, the answer is not simply to post more on Instagram or chase more customers.

The problem is structural.

So before you ask, “How do I sell more?”, there is a less exciting but much more useful question to ask first: how does this business actually make money?

The core rule is simple: growth amplifies the economics you already have. If the structure is weak, volume magnifies the weakness; if the structure is sound, volume has something worth amplifying.

Start with the basics.

Revenue is what comes in.

Margin is what remains after the costs required to create and deliver the sale.

And a small difference in margin can become a very large difference when it is multiplied across hundreds or thousands of transactions.

Saving fifty cents on something you sell ten times may not change much.

Saving fifty cents on something you sell ten thousand times can change the economics of the entire business.

That is why a business model is not just your final price.

It is the relationship between price, costs, channel, logistics, support, inventory, time, and the way value reaches the customer.

And this is where many businesses make a predictable mistake.

They assume that changing channel simply means moving the same business from one place to another.

For example: close the physical shop, move online, save money.

Maybe.

But online creates different costs and different responsibilities.

You need accurate inventory.

You need product pages that answer questions a salesperson used to answer in person.

You need photographs, descriptions, payment systems, packaging, shipping, customer support, and a process for returns.

You may save on rent and still spend more on acquisition, fulfilment, software, and service.

Digital does not mean free.

It means a different economic structure.

That distinction matters because the strongest business models are rarely built by treating the product as sacred.

They are built by understanding the problem the customer is trying to solve and then asking what forms of delivery make economic sense.

Imagine you sell a planner.

You can sell the planner as a physical object.

But the planner may also reveal a deeper customer problem: people do not simply want paper. They want to plan better, feel less overwhelmed, and make progress on the things that matter to them.

The lesson is not to multiply formats for their own sake. Stay loyal to the customer problem, then choose the delivery format that solves it without weakening the economics.

That brings us to scalability.

The word sounds technical, but the first diagnostic question is extremely simple.

If you stop working for one hour, does your revenue stop for one hour too?

If the answer is always yes, your model is tightly connected to your personal time.

That is not automatically bad.

One-to-one consulting can be an excellent business.

A designer, coach, lawyer, consultant, or specialist can create a strong company around high-value personal work.

But the model has a physical constraint: there are only so many hours in a day.

A recorded course has a different relationship between time and revenue.

You create it once, improve it over time, and more than one customer can use it without requiring the same number of new delivery hours from you every time.

That does not make a course superior to consulting.

It makes the economics different.

It may be cheaper for the customer, easier to distribute, and capable of separating at least part of your revenue from your available hours.

The useful question is therefore not, “How do I automate everything?”

It is: which parts of the value really need me, and which parts can be documented, standardised, recorded, productised, or delivered repeatedly without recreating them from zero?

Often the strongest model is a combination.

One part standardised.

One part personalised.

One part designed to attract new customers.

One part designed to create margin.

One part designed to create continuity.

Think about a consultant who spends the first two hours of every new project explaining the same methodology.

Those two hours may feel like personalised work, but perhaps the explanation itself does not need to be rebuilt each time.

It could become a structured onboarding, a video, a worksheet, a diagnostic, or a repeatable process.

The human time can then be reserved for interpretation, judgement, and decisions where the customer benefits from real expertise.

That is a much more useful form of scalability than trying to remove the human element from everything.

Now there is another test that every business eventually has to pass.

Will the model that works today still work when technology and customer behaviour change?

Blockbuster is a useful example because the underlying customer desire did not disappear.

People still wanted to watch films at home.

What changed was how they wanted access to them.

Netflix did not invent the desire to watch a movie on the sofa.

It changed the delivery model.

The broader lesson is not simply that one famous company failed and another succeeded.

It is that large, established businesses can become vulnerable when they defend a format more aggressively than they defend the customer need.

When the way people want to buy, use, or receive value changes, the business model has to be able to react.

So here is a practical way to look at your own business without starting from the product.

Take a sheet of paper and write down five things.

First: how does money enter the business?

Second: which costs increase every time you make another sale?

Third: which costs exist even when you sell nothing?

Fourth: how much human time is required to deliver what you promised?

And fifth: which part of the business could continue to create value without another hour of your personal time?

This exercise matters because we often look at a business through the product, while the model actually lives in the relationships between revenue, cost, time, delivery, and customer behaviour.

Then choose one product or service and calculate the economics of a single sale.

What do you charge?

What disappears in production, payment fees, commissions, shipping, support, refunds, software, and delivery time?

Which of those costs are obvious, and which have become invisible because you are used to paying them?

Then ask a simple stress-test question.

If sales doubled tomorrow, would the business definitely become healthier?

If doubling sales also doubles chaos, support workload, fulfilment problems, and your own working hours while leaving almost no additional margin, growth may amplify the weakness instead of solving it.

Next, look at time.

List the activities without which you currently cannot invoice or complete a sale.

If every additional customer always requires a completely new block of your time, you know exactly where the capacity ceiling sits.

Again, that may be acceptable.

But it should be a conscious choice, not something you discover only after demand increases.

Then look at the offer itself.

Is something you currently treat as an accessory valuable enough to become an offer on its own?

Is there a service you rebuild manually every time even though one part could be standardised?

Could a low-friction product become the entry point to a deeper service?

Could a high-touch service reveal a tool or process that many more people could use?

Do not make all of these changes at once.

Choose one hypothesis.

Maybe you can improve margin without making the customer experience worse.

Maybe one repetitive part of delivery can move out of your personal time.

Maybe customers need a different entry point into the same problem you already solve.

Make one change, watch what happens, and measure something simple for a few weeks.

Margin per sale.

Hours required for delivery.

Percentage of revenue that does not require one-to-one work.

Return rate.

Support time.

Choose the metric that tells you whether the structure is actually improving.

A business model does not become better because you collect more ideas.

It becomes better when you understand which levers genuinely change cost, time, value, and customer behaviour.

And there is one final question worth keeping in front of you.

Are you protecting the customer need, or are you protecting the way you have always served it?

Because a good business model is not a formula you choose once and keep forever.

It is a structure you understand well enough to question before the market forces you to change it.
