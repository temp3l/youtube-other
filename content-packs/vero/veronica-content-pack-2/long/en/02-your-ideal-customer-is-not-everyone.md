If your ideal customer is “anyone who could buy,” your problem is not lack of reach. It is lack of a decision. Without that decision, your title, examples, channels, and offer become generic. In this episode, we are not building a decorative persona. We are building a customer definition that actually changes what you say, where you say it, and which behavior tells you that the right person is paying attention.

Use a three-part test: need, context, signal. What does the customer need, in which situation does that need become urgent, and what observable behavior proves the interest is real? If a persona cannot improve one of those decisions, it is too vague.

You have avoided making a choice.

And when you do not choose who the message is for, it becomes almost impossible to know what to say, where to say it, or even which part of the product deserves attention.

Start with something simple.

A pair of earrings is an object.

But someone may buy those earrings because they want to feel more attractive, finish an outfit, express their taste, or give themselves a small reward.

That means age, city, and job title are not enough to understand a customer.

You need to understand the need and the emotion behind the decision.

Two people can buy exactly the same product for completely different reasons.

And when the reason changes, the message should change too.

Before you rewrite your communication, rebuild your website, or invent another product, stop and look at the person in front of you.

What are they thinking?

What are they feeling?

What are they afraid of?

What do they want?

What are they trying to make easier in their life?

Then ask a very practical question: where are they?

Not where you like to post.

Not where someone told you every business “has to be.”

Where does this customer actually spend attention?

Choosing Instagram because it happens to feel like the interesting platform of the moment is not a strategy.

If the person you need to reach spends more time on Facebook, reads newsletters, participates in groups, shops in physical stores, attends events, or uses another channel entirely, that is where the relationship needs to be built.

The channel comes after the customer.

Not the other way around.

This sounds obvious, yet businesses reverse the order all the time.

They open TikTok and only then try to decide what to say.

They launch a newsletter and only then look for someone to send it to.

They create an account on every platform and eventually discover they do not have enough time to manage even one of them properly.

The useful question is: where can I meet this person at a moment when my message could actually matter?

That is where buyer personas can help.

A buyer persona is not a real person.

It is a simplification.

A model.

Its purpose is to make a group of customers concrete enough to improve decisions.

What should I say?

Which example will make sense immediately?

Which objection should I anticipate?

Where should the message appear?

Think about a product like Red Bull.

Saying, “our target is everyone who can drink an energy drink,” does not tell you very much.

Now picture a student studying late at night.

The context changes immediately.

The moment changes.

The location changes.

The language changes.

Even where it would make sense for the product or the message to appear becomes clearer.

That simplified persona is useful not because it perfectly describes every customer, but because it forces you to make more precise choices.

There is an important warning here.

Do not turn buyer personas into marketing astrology.

You do not need thirty decorative facts about the name of the customer’s dog, their favorite color, or where they eat on Saturday night if none of those facts changes a decision.

A good persona changes your behavior.

If knowing that your customer is an extremely busy professional changes the length of the content, the way you deliver the service, or when you communicate, that information is useful.

If it changes nothing, it is probably not a priority.

And once you have real customers, start replacing assumptions with observed behavior.

That is where profiling and segmentation become genuinely useful.

You might have one group that bought a particular product.

Another group opens almost every newsletter but never buys.

Another repeatedly clicks on one topic.

Another belongs to a certain kind of business and has also taken a specific action.

When you combine declared characteristics with what people actually do, segmentation becomes more actionable.

You are no longer saying only, “this person is a woman between thirty and forty.”

You can say, “this person has shown repeated interest in this topic, took this action, bought this product, and ignored that one.”

Now you have information that can change communication.

But segmentation does not mean dividing your audience into smaller and smaller groups until every customer has a segment of one.

It means removing enough noise to make a better decision.

Try a simple exercise.

Pick one product or service and answer five questions.

What need is the person trying to solve?

What emotion sits behind it?

In what concrete situation does that need appear?

Where can you reach them?

What real behavior shows you that this interest exists?

If you cannot answer those questions, you probably do not have an advertising problem yet.

You have a definition problem.

Because an ideal customer is not there to make the marketing deck look sophisticated.

It is there to change a decision.

The message.

The channel.

The product.

The timing.

The segmentation.

There is another easy test for whether your buyer persona is useful or just a fictional character.

Take one real decision you have to make this week.

Which content should you publish?

Which product should you highlight?

Which channel deserves your attention?

Which objection should you address?

Now look at the description of your ideal customer.

Does it actually help you decide?

If the answer is no, you probably wrote too much decorative information and too little operational information.

Knowing that someone is thirty-seven and lives in a large city is not automatically useful.

It becomes useful when that characteristic changes the need, the behavior, the context, or where you can reach them.

That is why a buyer persona should remain a simplification, not become a caricature.

Then compare your hypothesis with behavior.

People can be grouped by profile characteristics, but also by what they do.

They open a newsletter.

They click on a topic.

They buy a category.

They respond to a piece of content.

They show repeated interest in a particular problem.

Those signals help you move from “I think this person is interested” to “I can see behavior that is consistent with this interest.”

And you can combine the two: profile plus behavior.

Not to build absurd surveillance around your customers, but to make communication more relevant.

If one group responds to one problem and another responds to something different, you do not have to speak to both in exactly the same way.

This is why “everyone” is such an expensive target.

When you try to speak to everyone at once, you remove details.

You remove tension.

You remove specific examples.

You remove context.

Eventually the message becomes generic enough that it feels written for no one.

So build your customer definition in three layers.

First, the need: what are they trying to achieve or avoid?

Second, the context: when and where does that need appear?

Third, the behavior: what observable signals tell you that the interest is real?

Only then add demographic characteristics that genuinely change a decision.

Now the buyer persona stops being a presentation document and becomes an operating tool.

And you stop selecting channels before selecting the person.

You do not need to be everywhere.

You need to be relevant in the place where the right customer can actually encounter you.

You can also validate the definition by talking to customers you already have. Do not only ask whether they like the product. Ask how they describe the problem, why they chose you, what they were comparing, and where they first found you.

The actual language of customers can correct a persona that was built entirely inside your own head.

If you discover that the purchase motivation is different from what you assumed, do not defend the profile. Update it.

And when a pattern appears, turn it into one concrete business decision: rewrite a message, leave a channel, create a piece of content, or treat one segment differently.

Analysis creates value only when it changes what the business does.

Keep a one-line decision log: “Because this is our customer, we changed this message, channel, example, objection, or offer.” If the definition never produces a sentence like that, it is still too decorative.

Once this is clear, you can move to the next question: what does this person believe they are actually buying?

Knowing the customer is not enough.

You also have to translate the value of your product into a promise that makes sense in their life.
