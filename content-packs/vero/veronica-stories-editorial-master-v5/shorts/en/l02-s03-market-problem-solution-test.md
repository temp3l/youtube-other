If your positioning feels vague, run this three-part test. First: which market do you understand well enough to describe specifically? Second: what problem does that group already recognize in its own words? Third: how does your solution connect directly to that problem?

If one answer is fuzzy, the whole position becomes fuzzy.

Most businesses reverse the sequence. They start with a package, a method, or a feature they want to sell, then search for people who might want it. Strong positioning moves in the other direction. Start with a real group. Listen to what they are experiencing. Describe the problem so clearly that they think, “Yes, that is exactly what is happening.” Then show why your solution is the logical response.

For example, “marketing for businesses” tells me almost nothing. “Helping independent consultants turn confusing expertise into an offer clients understand” connects a market, a problem, and a solution.

Now pressure-test it. Can you name five real people or companies that fit the market? Can they recognize the problem without you educating them for twenty minutes? Can you explain the solution without relying on internal jargon? If not, the position may still be theoretical.

The buyer should recognize themselves before you ask them to value what you sell. Market, problem, solution. If those three click together quickly, your positioning becomes much easier to understand and much easier to remember.
