A niche is not a contract you sign for the rest of your career. It is a market-entry strategy.

When nobody knows you yet, breadth creates a difficult question: why should this particular customer remember you? A focused niche gives you an easier answer. You can build examples around one kind of buyer, solve recurring problems in public, collect relevant proof, and make referrals simple.

That creates a foothold.

The important part comes next: expansion should follow evidence, not anxiety.

Suppose you become known for helping independent hotels improve direct bookings. Over time, boutique resorts start asking for the same work. Then serviced apartments notice that the underlying problem applies to them too. You can expand because the market is already pulling the expertise into adjacent categories.

That is different from starting with “I help hospitality businesses with growth.” The broad version asks the audience to figure out where you are strongest. The focused version gives them a clear entry point, then lets demand reveal where the position can travel.

So do not ask, “What niche can I tolerate forever?”

Ask, “Where can I become unusually relevant first?”

Build proof there. Watch which adjacent customers arrive. Expand one step at a time without erasing the association that created the demand.

A niche is a wedge. Use it to enter the market, not to imprison the business.
