You can be better than the person who wins the client — and still lose.

The reason is uncomfortable: customers cannot inspect your expertise directly. They judge the signals around it — your offer, your content, your reputation, your website, and what other people remember about you.

That gap between real expertise and perceived expertise is where positioning begins.

You can be extremely competent and still be perceived as one option among many. You can also create a very strong perception around a specific expertise, so that people immediately associate you with a particular result. In practical terms, that perception affects whether people notice you, trust you, contact you, and accept the price of your work.

Think about what happens when someone needs a very specific consultation. They usually do not want to spend weeks comparing every person in the market. They want to identify the person who appears to be the strongest fit for that problem.

The question is not only, “Who is good?”

The question becomes, “Who is the person for this?”

That is why being good at your job is necessary, but not sufficient.

If you sell a service, run a shop, manage a hotel, operate a beauty business, teach, consult, or create products, your work enters the customer’s mind before the customer enters your world. The customer has already formed an impression. They have seen how you present yourself, what you talk about, what you appear to specialize in, and how consistent those signals are.

This is perceptual positioning.

And it is easy to underestimate because it feels less concrete than improving your technical skills. You can take a course, learn a method, improve your product, or practice your craft. Positioning is different. It asks you to think about what another person understands when they encounter you.

Suppose you are a consultant and you describe yourself as someone who helps with strategy, communication, productivity, branding, social media, business development, websites, and a little bit of sales.

Everything may be true.

But what should the customer remember?

Now compare that with someone who is repeatedly associated with one recognizable area of expertise. Their message is easier to store in memory. Their name becomes easier to retrieve when the corresponding problem appears.

This does not mean you can only know one thing.

It means the market needs a clear doorway into your expertise.

A useful way to test your positioning is to ask a simple question: if somebody who knows your work had to introduce you in one sentence, what would they say?

Would they say, “She does lots of things”?

Or would they say, “She is the person you call when you need this specific thing”?

The second description is much more powerful.

But positioning is not built by declaring yourself an expert. Saying “I am an expert” does not make the market believe it. Recognition has to emerge from what people repeatedly see.

Your offer matters.

Your communication matters.

Your website matters.

Your social presence matters.

The topics you discuss matter.

The environments in which people encounter you matter.

Even the way your professional story is presented can influence how people interpret your expertise.

The strongest signals are coherent. They reinforce one another.

If your content says one thing, your offer says another, and your public presence says something else again, the customer has to do the work of understanding who you are. Most people will not do that work. They will simply move on to someone easier to understand.

This is why positioning is closely connected to perceived value.

When your expertise is easy to recognize and the evidence around it is coherent, people can understand why they should choose you. And when people understand the value clearly, price is no longer the only visible difference between you and the alternatives.

There is an important warning here.

Perceived value is not a substitute for real value.

The goal is not to manufacture an image that your work cannot support. The goal is to make your actual expertise legible to the market.

Start with what you genuinely do well.

Then ask what part of that expertise should become recognizable.

What problem do you want associated with your name?

Which kind of customer should recognize you?

What evidence can they see before they ever speak with you?

And are your public signals reinforcing the same idea often enough for people to remember it?

This is where many professionals make a mistake. They assume that good work will automatically communicate itself.

Sometimes it does.

Often it does not.

People cannot value what they do not understand. They cannot remember a position that was never made clear. And they cannot associate you with a problem if your communication constantly changes direction.

So if you are already good at your work, the next question is not simply how to become even better.

Ask how your expertise is being perceived.

Ask what people remember.

Ask what category they place you in.

And ask whether that category matches the work you actually want to sell.

Because the objective of positioning is not to look important.

It is to become recognizable for the right thing.

When that happens, people no longer have to decode what you do.

They already know why they should come to you.

There is one final exercise worth doing.

Write down three words you believe describe your professional value. Then ask a client, colleague, or follower to describe what they think you are known for without showing them your answer.

The difference between those two answers is useful information.

If they match, your positioning is already communicating something clearly.

If they do not, do not treat that as an insult. Treat it as a signal. Your market is telling you what it currently sees.

You can then decide whether to reinforce that perception or deliberately change it.

That is why positioning is not a one-time branding exercise. It is something you observe, test, and refine as your work evolves.
